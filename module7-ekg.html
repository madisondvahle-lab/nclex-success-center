Targeted Deep Dive · Module 7

EKG & Cardiac Rhythms

NCLEX does not require you to become a telemetry expert. Your job is to recognize the important rhythm, assess the patient, and choose the safest next action.

PATIENT → PULSE → RHYTHM → ACTION

How to read the strip

Use the same sequence every time. Consistency makes rhythm questions much easier.

1 · RATE
2 · RHYTHM
3 · P WAVES
4 · QRS
5 · PATIENT
NCLEX priority: Is the patient stable? Do they have a pulse? What needs to happen first?

Sinus rhythms

Sinus rhythms originate from the SA node. Focus first on the rate and whether the patient is symptomatic.

RHYTHM 01

Normal Sinus Rhythm

👁 What gives it away?
Regular + normal rate + one P before every QRS. Everything is conducting in the expected sequence.
See

Rate 60–100, regular rhythm, P before every QRS.

Think

Normal SA-node conduction.

Do

No rhythm-specific emergency intervention.

RHYTHM 02

Sinus Bradycardia

👁 What gives it away?
It looks like normal sinus rhythm, just slow. Rate is under 60. The NCLEX question is whether the patient is symptomatic.
See

Normal sinus pattern with rate under 60.

Think

Is the patient symptomatic?

Do

Monitor if stable. Symptomatic bradycardia may require atropine and pacing.

RHYTHM 03

Sinus Tachycardia

👁 What gives it away?
It looks like normal sinus rhythm, just fast. Rate is over 100. Look for the reason: pain, fever, hypovolemia, hypoxia, anxiety, or infection.
See

Normal sinus pattern with rate over 100.

Think

Why is the heart fast?

Do

Treat the cause such as pain, fever, hypovolemia, hypoxia, or infection.

Your Turn · Checkpoint 1

Sinus Rhythms

1. A client has a heart rate of 48/min but is alert, normotensive, and denies dizziness or chest discomfort. Which action is best?

Check answer
Continue assessment and monitoring.

NCLEX does not treat the number alone. Sinus bradycardia becomes urgent when it is causing poor perfusion or symptoms.

2. A postoperative client develops sinus tachycardia at 124/min. Which finding should the nurse investigate first?

Check answer
The cause of the tachycardia.

Think pain, fever, bleeding, hypovolemia, hypoxia, or infection. Sinus tachycardia is often a response to another problem.

Atrial rhythms

RHYTHM 04

Atrial Fibrillation

👁 What gives it away?
Irregularly irregular + no consistent P waves. On NCLEX, immediately connect A-fib with blood stasis, clot formation, and stroke risk.
See

Irregularly irregular rhythm with no consistent P waves.

Think

Atrial quivering creates blood stasis and increases embolic stroke risk.

Do

Think rate control, anticoagulation concepts, and synchronized cardioversion if unstable.

RHYTHM 05

Atrial Flutter

👁 What gives it away?
Sawtooth flutter waves. The repeating atrial activity is the visual clue that separates flutter from A-fib.
See

Classic repeating sawtooth flutter waves.

Think

Rapid organized atrial activity.

Do

Rate control, anticoagulation when indicated, cardioversion if unstable.

RHYTHM 06

SVT

👁 What gives it away?
Very fast + regular + narrow. P waves may disappear into the rapid rhythm. Then ask: stable or unstable?
See

Very fast, usually regular, narrow-complex rhythm. P waves may be hidden.

Do

Stable: vagal maneuvers or adenosine when appropriate. Unstable: synchronized cardioversion.

Your Turn · Checkpoint 2

Atrial Rhythms

3. Rhythm 04 is noted on the monitor. Which complication is the nurse most concerned about?

Check answer
Embolic stroke.

Atrial fibrillation causes ineffective atrial contraction and blood stasis, increasing thromboembolic risk.

4. Which finding most strongly suggests Rhythm 05 rather than Rhythm 04?

Check answer
Repeating sawtooth atrial waves.

Atrial flutter has organized flutter waves. A-fib is irregularly irregular with no consistent P waves.

5. A client with SVT is hypotensive, confused, and reporting chest pain. Which intervention should the nurse anticipate?

Check answer
Synchronized cardioversion.

This is an unstable tachyarrhythmia with a pulse.

Ventricular rhythms

RHYTHM 07

PVCs

👁 What gives it away?
An early beat suddenly looks wide and bizarre. It arrives before the next expected normal beat because it originated in the ventricle.
See

Early, wide, bizarre ventricular beat.

Think

Assess oxygenation, ischemia, electrolytes, stimulants, and medications.

RHYTHM 08

Ventricular Tachycardia

👁 What gives it away?
FAST + WIDE + usually regular. Your next thought should not be “shock.” Your next thought is: DOES THE PATIENT HAVE A PULSE?
See

Fast, wide, usually regular ventricular rhythm.

First Question

Does the patient have a pulse?

Do

No pulse: CPR + defibrillation. Pulse but unstable: synchronized cardioversion.

RHYTHM 09

Ventricular Fibrillation

👁 What gives it away?
Chaotic electrical activity with no organized QRS complexes. This is a pulseless shockable rhythm: CPR + defibrillation.
See

Chaotic electrical activity with no organized QRS complexes.

Do

CPR + DEFIBRILLATE.

RHYTHM 10

Asystole

👁 What gives it away?
No meaningful ventricular electrical activity. Confirm the rhythm/leads, then CPR + epinephrine. Do not defibrillate.
Do

Confirm rhythm and leads, begin CPR, give epinephrine per arrest protocol.

Never

Do not defibrillate asystole.

RHYTHM 11

PEA

👁 What gives it away?
You cannot diagnose PEA from the strip alone. The monitor shows electrical activity, but the patient has no palpable pulse. That clinical finding is what makes it PEA.
See

The monitor can show organized electrical activity.

Critical Finding

The patient has no pulse.

Do

CPR + epinephrine. Search for reversible causes. Do not defibrillate.

Your Turn · Checkpoint 3

Ventricular Rhythms

6. The monitor suddenly shows Rhythm 08. What assessment must occur before choosing the treatment pathway?

Check answer
Check for a pulse.

V-tach with a pulse and pulseless V-tach are treated differently.

7. A client is unresponsive and pulseless with Rhythm 09. What is the priority intervention?

Check answer
Begin CPR and defibrillate as soon as possible.

V-fib is a shockable cardiac-arrest rhythm.

8. A client is pulseless and the monitor shows Rhythm 10. Which action is inappropriate?

Check answer
Defibrillation.

Asystole is not shockable. Priorities are CPR, epinephrine, and evaluation for reversible causes.

9. The monitor shows organized electrical activity, but the client has no palpable pulse. Which rhythm should the nurse suspect?

Check answer
PEA.

PEA is defined clinically by electrical activity without a pulse. The ECG appearance alone does not diagnose it.

Heart blocks

RHYTHM 12

First-Degree AV Block

👁 What gives it away?
Every P still gets a QRS, but the PR interval is prolonged and stays prolonged. Think delayed conduction, not dropped conduction.

Every P wave conducts to a QRS, but the PR interval is prolonged and constant.

RHYTHM 13

Mobitz I · Wenckebach

👁 What gives it away?
Longer → longer → longer → DROP. The PR interval progressively lengthens until one QRS complex disappears.

Longer → longer → longer → DROP.

RHYTHM 14

Mobitz II

👁 What gives it away?
The PR interval stays consistent, then a QRS suddenly drops. Mobitz II is more dangerous because it can progress to complete heart block.

PR intervals stay consistent, but some P waves suddenly are not followed by QRS complexes.

More dangerous. Think pacing.

RHYTHM 15

Third-Degree AV Block

👁 What gives it away?
P waves and QRS complexes have no consistent relationship. The atria and ventricles are marching independently. Think complete heart block + pacing.

P waves and QRS complexes have no consistent relationship.

Think complete heart block and pacing.

Your Turn · Checkpoint 4

Heart Blocks

10. Which feature best distinguishes Rhythm 13 from Rhythm 14?

Check answer
Rhythm 13 progressively lengthens the PR interval before a QRS drops.

Mobitz I lengthens progressively. Mobitz II has consistent PR intervals with unexpected dropped QRS complexes.

11. Which heart block should make the nurse most concerned about progression to complete heart block?

Check answer
Mobitz II.

Second-degree AV block Type II is more unstable and often requires pacing.

12. Rhythm 15 shows P waves and QRS complexes occurring independently. What treatment should the nurse anticipate if the client is symptomatic?

Check answer
Pacing.

Third-degree AV block is complete AV dissociation and can cause severe bradycardia and poor perfusion.

Shock or don't shock?

⚡ SHOCKABLE

  • Ventricular fibrillation
  • Pulseless ventricular tachycardia

CPR + DEFIBRILLATION

🚫 NOT SHOCKABLE

  • Asystole
  • PEA

CPR + EPINEPHRINE

No pulse + V-fib/V-tach = DEFIB
Pulse + unstable tachyarrhythmia = SYNCHRONIZED CARDIOVERSION

High-yield rhythm medications

Adenosine Think regular narrow-complex SVT. Rapid IV push followed immediately by saline flush.
Atropine Think symptomatic bradycardia.
Epinephrine Think cardiac arrest, including asystole and PEA.
Amiodarone Think ventricular dysrhythmias, including refractory V-fib/pulseless V-tach in the arrest algorithm.

NCLEX danger zone

Trap: “The monitor shows V-tach, so defibrillate.”
First determine whether the patient has a pulse.
Trap: “There is electrical activity, so there must be a pulse.”
PEA specifically disproves this.
Trap: “Asystole means shock immediately.”
Asystole is not shockable.
Trap: “Heart rate 48 means give atropine.”
Assess symptoms and perfusion first.
Final memory rule: PATIENT → PULSE → RHYTHM → ACTION
