Cardiovascular Disorders

Think Perfusion. Assess Early. Act Quickly. Keep the Patient Safe.

Phase 1 · Foundations

1 · Module Overview

Why this module matters: Cardiovascular questions are woven throughout NCLEX. You must recognize poor perfusion early, know the difference between left and right heart failure, identify shockable rhythms, and know the safest nursing action before the patient deteriorates. The NCLEX isn't testing whether you can diagnose every heart disease — it's testing whether you can recognize the danger and act.

🎯 What You'll Master

  • Cardiac output (CO = HR × SV) and what disrupts it
  • Left vs. right heart failure signs
  • Shockable vs. non-shockable rhythms
  • Acute MI recognition — typical AND atypical
  • Hypertensive urgency vs. emergency
  • Valvular disorders and their heart sounds
  • Cardiogenic shock: recognize and prioritize
  • Priority cardiac medications and their hold parameters

📊 NCLEX Weight

Cardiovascular is consistently one of the highest-yield content areas across NCLEX. Questions appear in physiological adaptation, pharmacology, and reduction of risk.

NCLEX Takeaway The NCLEX cares less about the diagnosis and more about whether you recognize poor perfusion early and know the safest nursing action before the patient deteriorates.

2 · Learning Objectives

  • 1Calculate cardiac output and identify four factors that affect it (HR, contractility, preload, afterload).
  • 2Differentiate left-sided from right-sided heart failure by signs and symptoms.
  • 3Classify cardiac rhythms as shockable vs. non-shockable and state the priority intervention for each.
  • 4Recognize both typical and atypical MI presentation and apply the "Time = Muscle" principle.
  • 5Distinguish hypertensive urgency from hypertensive emergency and identify priority nursing actions.
  • 6Identify signs of cardiogenic shock and prioritize nursing interventions.
  • 7Apply cardiac medication hold parameters (digoxin, beta-blockers, nitroglycerin, diuretics, anticoagulants).
  • 8Describe valvular disorders and their associated heart sounds.

EXTENSION — Cardiac Output & Heart Failure Deep Dive

CO = HR × SV

Cardiac Output = Heart Rate × Stroke Volume  |  Normal: 4–8 L/min

4 Factors That Affect CO:

  • Heart Rate — how fast the heart beats
  • Contractility — strength of the heart muscle
  • Preload — how much blood stretches the ventricle before contraction (volume)
  • Afterload — resistance the heart pumps against (SVR)
NCLEX Pearl Low cardiac output = poor perfusion. Signs: ↓ BP, cold/clammy skin, ↓ urine output (<30 mL/hr), altered LOC, weak/thready pulses, narrow pulse pressure. These all = CALL THE PROVIDER.

📊 HF by Ejection Fraction (EF)

  • HFrEF (EF <40%): Heart can't SQUEEZE — systolic dysfunction. Treat with ACEi, beta-blockers, diuretics.
  • HFpEF (EF ≥50%): Heart can't RELAX/FILL — diastolic dysfunction. Treat symptoms: diuretics, BP control.
  • HFmrEF (EF 41–49%): Mid-range — features of both.
  • EF = % of blood pumped out with each beat. Normal: 55–70%.

🏥 HF Priority Nursing Actions

  1. Auscultate lungs (crackles = fluid)
  2. Daily weights — same time, same scale, same clothing
  3. I&O every shift
  4. Monitor VS and O₂ saturation
  5. Assess edema (pitting?)
  6. Administer meds as ordered (diuretics, ACEi)
  7. Teach low-sodium diet (<2 g/day) and fluid restriction
  8. Report changes early — weight gain of 2–3 lbs in 1 day or 5 lbs in 1 week
SNS & RAAS Activation in HF When CO falls → SNS activates (↑HR, ↑contractility, vasoconstriction) → RAAS activates (kidneys retain Na+ & water → ↑blood volume → ↑BP). This helps SHORT-TERM but long-term makes HF WORSE — that's why ACE inhibitors and beta-blockers (which block these responses) improve survival in HFrEF.
Rule #1: SHOCK IT if it's shockable. CPR if it's not. You must know which is which.

⚡ SHOCKABLE (Defibrillate)

  • Ventricular Fibrillation (VF) — chaotic, no pulse
  • Pulseless Ventricular Tachycardia (pVT) — fast, no pulse
Action: Immediate defibrillation (unsynchronized cardioversion) + CPR. VF and pulseless VT = SHOCK FIRST.

🔵 NON-SHOCKABLE (CPR only)

  • Pulseless Electrical Activity (PEA) — organized rhythm, no pulse
  • Asystole — flatline, no electrical activity
Action: High-quality CPR + epinephrine q3–5 min + find/treat reversible causes (H's & T's).

Time = Muscle. Every minute of untreated MI = more myocardium dies. Goal: door-to-balloon <90 min for STEMI.

⚠️ Typical MI Symptoms

  • Crushing, pressure chest pain (may radiate jaw/arm)
  • Diaphoresis (sudden sweating)
  • Nausea/vomiting
  • Shortness of breath
  • Pallor, anxiety ("sense of doom")

🌸 Atypical MI Symptoms (Women/Elderly/Diabetics)

  • Fatigue (only symptom sometimes)
  • Nausea without chest pain
  • Indigestion/heartburn feeling
  • Shortness of breath alone
  • Back or jaw pain without chest pain
NCLEX Trap: Women having MIs are frequently undertreated because their symptoms are atypical. If a middle-aged woman presents with fatigue, nausea, and jaw pain — think MI until proven otherwise.

🚨 Priority Actions for Suspected MI — ALWAYS:

  1. ABCs — airway, breathing, circulation first
  2. Vital signs — BP, HR, RR, O₂ sat
  3. 12-Lead ECG — within 10 minutes of arrival (ST elevation = STEMI)
  4. Troponin — most specific cardiac biomarker (rises in 3–6 hours)
  5. IV access — for medication administration
  6. O₂ — if SpO₂ <90%
  7. Aspirin — 325 mg chewed (unless contraindicated)
  8. Nitroglycerin — SL for chest pain (hold if SBP <90 or if patient took sildenafil/tadalafil)
  9. Notify provider/activate cath lab for STEMI
MONA — Old School Memory Aid Morphine · Oxygen · Nitroglycerin · Aspirin. MONA is not always the current treatment order (oxygen only if SpO₂ <90%, morphine use is now debated in STEMI), but still useful for NCLEX recall. The ABCs + ECG + Troponin + IV access framework is the priority.

🎯 Signs of Target Organ Damage (Emergency)

  • Chest pain / acute MI
  • Stroke symptoms (slurred speech, facial droop)
  • Hypertensive encephalopathy (severe HA, confusion)
  • Acute kidney injury (↑ Cr, ↓ UO)
  • Aortic dissection (tearing chest/back pain)
  • Pulmonary edema
  • Retinal hemorrhage / papilledema

💊 First-Line HTN Medications

  • Thiazide diuretics (HCTZ) — most patients
  • ACE inhibitors / ARBs — DM, CKD, HF
  • CCBs (amlodipine) — elderly, isolated systolic HTN
  • Beta-blockers — post-MI, HFrEF, A-fib (not first-line for HTN alone)
Never reduce BP too fast in emergency — ischemia risk to brain, heart, kidneys.
Cardiogenic Shock = Heart can't pump enough blood to meet the body's needs. Most common cause: massive MI. Think pump failure.

🔻 Signs of Cardiogenic Shock

  • Hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg)
  • Cold, clammy, mottled skin
  • Weak, thready pulses
  • Altered LOC / confusion
  • Low urine output (<30 mL/hr)
  • Narrow pulse pressure
  • Pulmonary crackles (fluid backing up)
  • Tachycardia (compensatory)

🏥 Priority Interventions — Cardiogenic Shock

  1. Maintain oxygenation — O₂, possible intubation
  2. IV access × 2 — large bore
  3. Vasopressors as prescribed (dopamine, norepinephrine) to maintain perfusion pressure
  4. Monitor hemodynamics closely (arterial line, CVP, pulmonary artery catheter)
  5. Prepare for possible IABP (intra-aortic balloon pump) or PCI
  6. Strict I&O — UO ≥30 mL/hr is the goal
  7. Position: HOB low (unless pulmonary edema); legs elevated cautiously
  8. Address underlying cause (MI → reperfusion therapy)
Perfusion Assessment (NCLEX favorite)
• Skin color & temperature
• Capillary refill <3 sec = normal
• Peripheral pulses
• Level of consciousness
• Urine output >30 mL/hr
• BP and MAP
• Heart rate & rhythm

Poor perfusion = priority finding. Act immediately.

🔊 Heart Sounds

  • S1 ("Lub") — closure of mitral & tricuspid valves; start of systole — NORMAL
  • S2 ("Dub") — closure of aortic & pulmonic valves; end of systole — NORMAL
  • S3 ("Ken-tucky") — early diastole; low-pitched; ABNORMAL in adults = ventricular overload / HF. Normal in children & athletes.
  • S4 ("Ten-nes-see") — late diastole; low-pitched; ALWAYS pathologic = stiff ventricle, HTN, MI

📍 Auscultation Points (APTM)

  • Aortic — 2nd ICS, right sternal border
  • Pulmonic — 2nd ICS, left sternal border
  • Tricuspid — 4th ICS, left sternal border
  • Mitral (Apex) — 5th ICS, midclavicular line
S1, S2 = Normal. S3, S4 = May Not Be.

6 · Memory Tricks

Mnemonic

Left = Lungs · Right = Rest

Left HF → fluid backs into the LUNGS (crackles, SOB, pink frothy sputum). Right HF → fluid backs into the REST of the body (JVD, peripheral edema, ascites, hepatomegaly).

Mnemonic

SHOCK IT vs. CPR IT

VF + pVT = SHOCK IT (shockable). PEA + Asystole = CPR IT (not shockable). There are only 2 shockable rhythms. Everything else = CPR + epinephrine.

Mnemonic

Time = Muscle (for MI)

Every minute without reperfusion = more cardiomyocytes die. Door-to-balloon goal: <90 min for STEMI. Your job: 12-lead ECG within 10 minutes, activate the cath lab.

Mnemonic

S1S2 = Normal · S3S4 = May Not Be

S3 = fluid overload/HF (abnormal in adults). S4 = stiff ventricle from HTN or MI (always pathologic). Hear S3 → think HF.

Trick

CO = HR × SV — The Foundation

If HR ↓ or SV ↓ → CO ↓ → perfusion ↓. If CO is low, everything suffers: brain (LOC ↓), kidneys (UO ↓), skin (cold/clammy). These are your assessment targets.

Trick

Aortic Stenosis Triad = "SAS"

Syncope · Angina · Shortness of breath. This classic triad in a patient with a systolic murmur radiating to the carotids = severe aortic stenosis → valve replacement needed.

Trick

Nitro Rules — 3 Before Call

For stable angina: SL nitro q5 min × 3 doses. If pain NOT relieved after 3 doses → call 911/provider immediately. HOLD nitro if SBP <90 OR patient took sildenafil/tadalafil within 24–48 hrs (severe hypotension).

Trick

Daily Weights = Fluid Balance

Weight gain of 2–3 lbs in 1 day or 5 lbs in 1 week = fluid retention → call provider. Same time, same scale, same amount of clothing. This is the earliest sign of worsening HF.

7 · NCLEX Pearls

  • ✅ Troponin does NOT rise immediately — initial troponin can be normal even in an MI. Always get serial troponins (q6h × 3).
  • ✅ Left HF = Lungs (crackles, SOB). Right HF = Rest of body (JVD, edema, ascites). When both sides fail = biventricular failure = signs of both.
  • ✅ VF and pulseless VT = the only 2 shockable rhythms. Defibrillate immediately.
  • ✅ Hold nitroglycerin if SBP <90 OR patient took sildenafil (Viagra) or tadalafil (Cialis) — combination causes severe, potentially fatal hypotension.
  • ✅ Digoxin toxicity is worsened by hypokalemia — always check K+ before giving digoxin. Hold if apical pulse <60.
  • ✅ A-fib = clot risk in the atria → stroke. All A-fib patients need anticoagulation assessment (CHADS-VASc score).
  • ✅ Daily weights are the EARLIEST sign of fluid retention in HF — 2–3 lbs/day or 5 lbs/week = call provider.
  • ✅ Hypertensive emergency (with organ damage): reduce MAP by ≤25% in the first hour — NOT to normal. Too-rapid reduction = ischemia.
  • ✅ Aortic stenosis triad: syncope + angina + SOB = severe stenosis requiring intervention. Murmur radiates to carotids.
  • ✅ Weight gain in HF occurs BEFORE shortness of breath — this is why daily weights matter so much.
  • ✅ Edema ≠ CHF every time (can be from venous insufficiency, low albumin, lymphedema). Always assess for the full picture.
  • ✅ PVCs after an MI can be normal — don't panic. But frequent multifocal PVCs or R-on-T phenomenon = concern.

8 · NCLEX Traps

  • 🚫 Trap: Giving nitroglycerin to a patient who took sildenafil. NEVER — causes severe hypotension. Always ask about ED medications.
  • 🚫 Trap: Assuming the first troponin being normal rules out MI. Troponin takes 3–6 hours to rise. Serial testing is required.
  • 🚫 Trap: Treating edema as always meaning CHF. Edema has many causes. Assess lung sounds, weight trend, and clinical context.
  • 🚫 Trap: Reducing BP to normal in a hypertensive emergency. Reduce MAP ≤25% in the first hour — not all the way to normal (causes brain/heart/kidney ischemia).
  • 🚫 Trap: Thinking S3 is always pathologic. S3 is normal in children and athletes. Pathologic in adults ≥40 = suspect HF.
  • 🚫 Trap: Giving K+ supplements to a patient on spironolactone AND an ACE inhibitor. Both raise K+ → dangerous hyperkalemia.
  • 🚫 Trap: Not realizing women have atypical MI symptoms. Fatigue + nausea + jaw pain in a middle-aged woman = cardiac until proven otherwise.
  • 🚫 Trap: Choosing to defibrillate PEA or asystole. These are NOT shockable. CPR + epinephrine + find reversible cause.
  • 🚫 Trap: Giving digoxin when K+ is low without correcting it first. Hypokalemia potentiates digoxin toxicity → arrhythmias.
  • 🚫 Trap: Abruptly stopping a beta-blocker. Always taper — sudden cessation causes rebound tachycardia and may precipitate MI.

9 · Clinical Connections

🫀 Post-MI Patient

After an MI: serial troponins, continuous cardiac monitoring, watch for extension of infarct (new ST changes), arrhythmias (most common cause of death post-MI = V-fib), cardiogenic shock signs (↓BP, ↓UO, altered LOC). Beta-blockers and ACE inhibitors reduce mortality post-MI.

💧 HF Patient on Diuretics

Furosemide → ↓ K+ → potentiates digoxin toxicity. Always check K+ and replace. Daily weights are mandatory. Fluid restriction + low-sodium diet. Watch for orthostatic hypotension (fall risk after diuretics).

🔌 Patient with Pacemaker

Avoid MRI (unless MRI-conditional device). No strong magnets near device. Assess pacemaker function: is it capturing (each pacer spike followed by a complex)? Pacemaker syndrome: dizziness, fatigue, palpitations = device may need adjustment.

⚡ A-Fib Patient

Irregularly irregular rhythm, no distinct P waves. High stroke risk — anticoagulation is key. Rate control with beta-blockers/CCBs. Rhythm control with amiodarone. Cardioversion possible but must rule out atrial clot first (TEE or 3 weeks of anticoagulation).

🩺 Auscultation Finding

If you hear an S3 in an adult with dyspnea, crackles, and peripheral edema → HF until proven otherwise. If you hear a systolic murmur radiating to carotids with syncope + angina → aortic stenosis → do NOT exercise this patient, prepare for valve replacement discussion.

🧪 Critical Lab Values

  • K+ <3.0 → arrhythmia risk, hold digoxin, replace
  • BNP >500 → severe HF exacerbation
  • Troponin any elevation → MI until proven otherwise
  • INR >3.5 → hold warfarin, notify provider
  • aPTT >100 sec → heparin toxicity, reduce rate

10 · Priority Framework

❤️ NCLEX Cardiovascular Priority Order

  1. Airway/Breathing/Circulation (ABCs) — always first
  2. Is there a pulse? No → CPR immediately. Shockable rhythm → defibrillate.
  3. Is there poor perfusion? (↓BP, ↓UO, ↓LOC, cold/clammy) → notify provider, prepare vasopressors
  4. Chest pain? → 12-lead ECG within 10 min, troponin, O₂, IV, aspirin
  5. Assess before any cardiac medication → check hold parameters (HR, BP, K+, INR/aPTT as appropriate)
  6. Monitor response → VS, UO, lung sounds, LOC, daily weights
Intervene Immediately
• Pulseless patient → CPR + defibrillate (if shockable)
• VF or pulseless VT on monitor
• SBP <90 with shock signs
• New ST elevation on 12-lead
• Nitroglycerin contraindication (sildenafil use, SBP <90)
• Signs of digoxin toxicity (yellow halos, bradycardia, N/V)
Hold the Drug and Notify Provider
• Digoxin: HR <60 or K+ low
• Beta-blocker: HR <60 or SBP <90
• Nitroglycerin: SBP <90
• Anticoagulant: supratherapeutic INR/aPTT or active bleeding
• Diuretic: severe dehydration or severe electrolyte imbalance
• ACE inhibitor/ARB: angioedema or severe hypotension

11 · Medication Focus

Furosemide (Lasix)

Loop Diuretic
Action:Blocks Na-K-2Cl in loop of Henle → ↑ urine output → ↓ fluid overload
Uses:Heart failure, pulmonary edema, HTN, edema
Before:BP, K+, BUN/Cr, daily weight
Monitor:K+ (hypokalemia → arrhythmias, ↑ digoxin toxicity), BP (hypotension), UO
Teach:Daily weights; eat K+-rich foods or take supplement; fall risk (orthostatic hypotension); take in AM (nocturia)
⚠️ Monitor K+ closely · High-dose IV = ototoxicity · Sulfa allergy cross-reactivity possible

Metoprolol (Lopressor)

Beta-1 Selective Beta-Blocker
Action:Blocks beta-1 receptors → ↓HR, ↓contractility, ↓BP
Uses:HTN, angina, HFrEF, post-MI, A-fib rate control, SVT
Hold:HR <60 or SBP <90
Side Effects:Bradycardia, hypotension, fatigue, bronchoconstriction (less with beta-1 selective)
Teach:Do NOT stop abruptly (rebound tachycardia/MI); monitor HR daily; avoid cold medications with pseudoephedrine
⚠️ Never stop abruptly · Use with caution in asthma/COPD · Masks hypoglycemia symptoms in diabetics

Nitroglycerin (NTG)

Nitrate / Antianginal
Action:Vasodilates coronary arteries and veins → ↓ preload, ↓ afterload → ↓ O₂ demand
Uses:Angina (acute), ACS, HF (IV), HTN (IV)
SL Dosing:1 tab under tongue q5 min × 3; if pain unrelieved → call 911
Hold:SBP <90 or patient took sildenafil/tadalafil/vardenafil within 24–48 hrs
Teach:Store in dark glass bottle, away from heat; headache is common (vasodilation); sit/lie down after dose; burning under tongue = good (drug is potent)
⚠️ NEVER with sildenafil/tadalafil (Viagra/Cialis) → fatal hypotension · Hold if SBP <90

Lisinopril (Zestril)

ACE Inhibitor
Action:Inhibits ACE → ↓ angiotensin II → ↓ BP, ↓ afterload, ↓ aldosterone → ↓ Na/water retention
Uses:HTN, HFrEF, post-MI, diabetic nephropathy (renoprotective)
Monitor:BP (first-dose hypotension), K+ (hyperkalemia), BUN/Cr, dry cough
Stop If:Angioedema (swelling of lips, tongue, throat = airway emergency)
Teach:Change positions slowly; dry cough is common → switch to ARB if intolerable; avoid NSAIDs; teratogenic — report pregnancy
⚠️ Angioedema = STOP drug immediately · Teratogenic (Category D) · Hyperkalemia risk with K-sparing diuretics/ARBs

13 · NGN Case Studies

📋 Case 1: "He Says His Heart Is Racing and He Can't Breathe"

▼
Mr. Carter, 71 years old, is brought to the ED by his wife. He reports sudden onset of palpitations, shortness of breath, and mild chest discomfort for the past 2 hours. PMH: hypertension, type 2 diabetes. Vital signs: HR 148 (irregular), BP 104/68, RR 22, SpO₂ 91% on room air. His telemetry shows an irregularly irregular rhythm with no distinct P waves. He appears anxious and diaphoretic. He denies taking any medications for erectile dysfunction.
1. What rhythm does the nurse recognize, and what is the immediate nursing priority?
Atrial fibrillation (A-fib) — irregularly irregular with no distinct P waves. Immediate priorities: Apply O₂ (SpO₂ 91% is below goal of ≥94%), obtain 12-lead ECG, establish IV access, continuous cardiac monitoring, notify provider. This is hemodynamically borderline (BP 104/68) — prepare for possible rate control medications or cardioversion.
2. The provider orders diltiazem IV for rate control. What assessment must the nurse perform BEFORE administering?
Before diltiazem (a calcium channel blocker): check BP and HR. Diltiazem decreases HR and BP — with BP already at 104/68 and HR 148, administering it could cause dangerous hypotension and/or excessive bradycardia. The nurse should notify the provider of the current BP and confirm it is safe to proceed. Also check for heart block (contraindication) and assess for HF (diltiazem reduces contractility).
3. The team discusses cardioversion vs. anticoagulation first. Why does the sequence matter?
In A-fib that has lasted >48 hours (or unknown duration), blood can pool in the left atrial appendage and form a clot. Cardioverting the heart (restoring normal rhythm) can dislodge that clot → stroke. To safely cardiovert: either perform a transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) first to rule out clot, OR anticoagulate for at least 3 weeks prior and 4 weeks after cardioversion. If the patient is hemodynamically unstable → emergency cardioversion regardless, with anticoagulation after.
4. Before discharge, Mr. Carter is started on warfarin. What is the most important teaching point?
Most important: keep ALL INR follow-up appointments (INR goal 2–3 for A-fib). Additional teaching: use an electric razor; report unusual bleeding or bruising; maintain consistent (not eliminated) green vegetable intake; avoid NSAIDs (use acetaminophen instead); many drug interactions — always tell every provider he's on warfarin; carry a medical alert ID. Emphasize he's on anticoagulation because A-fib significantly increases stroke risk (5× higher).

📋 Case 2: "She Just Feels Tired — It's Probably Nothing"

▼
Ms. Rivera, 58 years old, presents to the ED with her daughter who says "mom's just been really tired and nauseated for the past few hours, and she has some jaw pain — we think it's her acid reflux acting up." PMH: type 2 diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia. She is post-menopausal. Vitals: HR 94, BP 142/88, RR 18, SpO₂ 96%. She denies crushing chest pain. She appears pale and slightly diaphoretic. The triage nurse documents "GI complaint" and places her in a lower-acuity area.
1. What does this clinical presentation suggest, and what is the error in the triage assessment?
This presentation is highly suspicious for an acute MI with ATYPICAL symptoms — common in women, diabetics, and older adults. Atypical MI symptoms include: fatigue, nausea, jaw/back pain, indigestion — WITHOUT classic crushing chest pain. The triage error: dismissing the presentation as GI without a 12-lead ECG. Risk factors (female, diabetic, hypertensive, hyperlipidemic, post-menopausal) combined with diaphoresis and pallor should have triggered immediate cardiac workup.
2. The nurse obtains a 12-lead ECG. It shows ST elevation in leads II, III, and aVF. What does this indicate and what happens next?
ST elevation in II, III, aVF = inferior wall STEMI (right coronary artery territory). This is a STEMI — time = muscle. Immediate actions: Activate the cardiac catheterization lab (door-to-balloon goal <90 min), obtain IV access ×2, administer aspirin 325 mg chewed (unless contraindicated), nitroglycerin SL (check BP first — hold if SBP <90), continuous cardiac monitoring, draw troponin/CBC/BMP/coagulation labs, notify the cath team, prepare patient for emergent PCI.
3. The initial troponin comes back normal. The provider wants to discharge the patient. What should the nurse do?
The nurse should advocate for the patient and NOT allow discharge based on a single normal troponin. Troponin takes 3–6 hours to rise after myocardial injury. A normal initial troponin does NOT rule out MI. The standard of care is serial troponins (q6h × 3) along with clinical picture and ECG changes. The ST elevation on ECG is already diagnostic for STEMI — action should have been taken immediately regardless of the troponin result. The nurse should respectfully escalate to the provider and charge nurse.
4. After PCI, Ms. Rivera is on the cardiac unit. What are the priority nursing assessments?
Post-PCI/post-MI nursing priorities:
• Continuous cardiac monitoring — watch for reperfusion arrhythmias (PVCs, V-fib — most common cause of death post-MI)
• Assess access site (femoral or radial): bleeding, hematoma, pulse check distal to site
• Serial troponins to peak and trend
• Monitor for cardiogenic shock: ↓BP, ↓UO, altered LOC, cold/clammy skin
• Administer ordered medications: dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin + P2Y12 inhibitor), beta-blocker, ACE inhibitor, statin
• Mobility restrictions per protocol (especially if femoral access)
• Begin cardiac rehabilitation education early
