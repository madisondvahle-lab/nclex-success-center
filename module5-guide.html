Respiratory Disorders

Assess Early. Intervene Quickly. Support Oxygenation. That's NCLEX Success.

Phase 1 · Foundations

1 · Module Overview

Why this module matters: The NCLEX isn't testing whether you know every respiratory disease. It's testing whether you can recognize respiratory decline early and take the safest action. Respiratory questions appear on almost every NCLEX — oxygenation is the most basic human need, and the nurse must act fast when it's compromised.

🎯 What the NCLEX Expects You to Know

  • Oxygen delivery devices and their FiO₂ ranges
  • Breath sounds — normal vs. abnormal
  • ABG interpretation (ROME method)
  • Chest tube management — do's and don'ts
  • Mechanical ventilation alarms and responses
  • COPD vs. asthma — key differences
  • Pneumonia — priority nursing care
  • Pulmonary embolism — recognize and act
  • Respiratory prioritization (A-O-V-C-T)

🔑 The NCLEX Respiratory Mindset

Think: A – O – V – C – T
Airway — keep it open
Oxygenation — get the O₂ in
Ventilation — get the CO₂ out
Circulation — support perfusion
Treat the Cause — solve the problem

Every respiratory NCLEX question can be approached with this framework.

5 · Deep Dive Lessons

🔑 ROME Method

  • Respiratory = Opposite: pH and PaCO₂ move in OPPOSITE directions (pH↓, CO₂↑ = resp acidosis)
  • Metabolic = Equal: pH and HCO₃⁻ move in the SAME direction (pH↓, HCO₃⁻↓ = metabolic acidosis)

⚖️ Compensation Rules

  • Respiratory problem → kidneys compensate (slow, hours to days)
  • Metabolic problem → lungs compensate (fast, minutes)
  • Uncompensated: only one value abnormal
  • Partially compensated: pH still abnormal, compensating value also abnormal
  • Fully compensated: pH normal, but both CO₂ and HCO₃⁻ are abnormal
COPD Patient ABG Baseline COPD patients chronically retain CO₂ (PaCO₂ may be 50–60 mmHg as their baseline). Their kidneys compensate by retaining HCO₃⁻ (fully compensated respiratory acidosis). A "normal" CO₂ in a COPD patient may actually mean they're tiring out. Know the patient's baseline.

🔒 Oxygen Safety Rules

✅ DO

  • Humidify when flow ≥4 L/min
  • Monitor COPD patients for CO₂ retention (target SpO₂ 88–92%)
  • Ensure proper tubing and flow
  • Treat O₂ as a MEDICATION — use it correctly

❌ NEVER

  • No smoking near O₂
  • No open flames
  • No petroleum products (use water-based lubricants)
  • No open sparks or electric appliances
  • Never give simple mask <6 L/min

🐷 COPD Memory: Blue Bloater vs. Pink Puffer

  • Blue Bloater (Chronic Bronchitis): cyanotic, overweight, chronic productive cough, CO₂ retention, edema
  • Pink Puffer (Emphysema): thin, pink, pursed-lip breathing, barrel chest, can't exhale trapped air, no cyanosis at rest
COPD O₂ Trap Do NOT give high-flow O₂ to COPD patients without caution. They rely on a hypoxic drive to breathe (their CO₂ receptors are blunted from chronic retention). High O₂ can suppress the drive to breathe → respiratory failure. Target SpO₂ 88–92%, use Venturi mask for precision.

🌬️ Incentive Spirometer — Best Prevention for Atelectasis

  • 10 breaths every hour while awake
  • Hold breath 3–5 seconds at peak inhalation
  • Small, frequent, DEEP breaths keep alveoli open
  • Cough after use to clear secretions
  • Used post-op and in any patient at risk for atelectasis
Pneumonia NCLEX Pearl: Listen to the LUNGS, watch the VITALS, and assess the OXYGENATION.

🔍 Expected Findings

  • Crackles / rhonchi over affected area
  • Fever / chills
  • Productive cough (purulent sputum)
  • Dyspnea, tachypnea
  • ↑ Respiratory rate
  • ↑ WBC (leukocytosis)
  • Chest X-ray: infiltrates/consolidation
  • Decreased breath sounds over affected lobe

🏥 Nursing Priorities (In Order)

  1. Airway — suction if needed, positioning
  2. Oxygenation — apply O₂, monitor SpO₂
  3. Antibiotics — obtain sputum culture FIRST, then give antibiotics
  4. Fluids — encourage fluids to thin secretions (unless contraindicated)
  5. Rest — position HOB 30–45°, semi-Fowler's
Culture BEFORE Antibiotics Always obtain sputum culture (and blood cultures if ordered) BEFORE administering the first antibiotic dose. This is the same rule as all infections — culture first, then treat empirically.
Suctioning Rules
✅ Preoxygenate with 100% O₂ first
✅ Suction while withdrawing (not inserting)
✅ Max 10–15 seconds per pass
✅ Hyperoxgenate after
❌ Never suction routinely
❌ Never suction longer than 15 sec
❌ Never insert catheter without preoxygenation

🚨 Pulmonary Embolism (PE)

Classic Signs (3 P's):

  • Pain — pleuritic chest pain (sharp, worse with breathing)
  • Panic — anxiety, restlessness, sense of doom
  • Pulmonary clot — hemoptysis, sudden SOB, tachycardia

Risk Factors:

  • Surgery (especially orthopedic), immobility, DVT history
  • Birth control pills, pregnancy, cancer
  • Virchow's Triad: stasis + hypercoagulability + vessel damage
Hypotension is a LATE sign. PE first sign may be only tachycardia or sudden anxiety.

🚨 PE Nursing Actions

  1. Position: HOB elevated, high Fowler's
  2. O₂ immediately (high flow)
  3. IV access × 2
  4. Notify provider STAT
  5. Continuous cardiac monitoring and pulse ox
  6. Anticipate: CT pulmonary angiography (gold standard diagnosis)
  7. Anticoagulation: heparin drip (check aPTT), then warfarin or DOAC
  8. Monitor for signs of shock (↓BP, ↓UO, altered LOC)

🫁 ARDS — Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome

  • Noncardiogenic pulmonary edema (heart is fine)
  • Severe hypoxemia — refractory to O₂ (PaO₂/FiO₂ <300)
  • Bilateral infiltrates on CXR
  • Decreased lung compliance (stiff lungs)
  • Causes: sepsis, aspiration, trauma, pneumonia, near-drowning
Refractory to O₂ = ARDS hallmark. Even with 100% O₂, SpO₂ stays low. These patients need mechanical ventilation with PEEP.
PEEP (positive end-expiratory pressure) keeps alveoli open between breaths. High PEEP can cause barotrauma — watch for pneumothorax.
Chest tube air can get IN but cannot get OUT — that's the whole design.

✅ Chest Tube — DO

  • Keep drainage system BELOW chest level
  • Maintain water seal (2 cm water)
  • Assess drainage amount, color, character every shift
  • Secure all tubing connections
  • Change dressing using sterile technique
  • Monitor for tidaling (fluctuation in water seal with breathing = normal)
  • Bubbling in water seal = air leak (may be normal initially, then should stop)

❌ Chest Tube — NEVER

  • NEVER clamp a chest tube (air/fluid can't escape → tension pneumothorax)
  • NEVER strip/milk tubing (creates excessive negative pressure)
  • NEVER raise drainage system above chest level (fluid drains back)
  • NEVER remove without a physician order
  • If tube accidentally disconnects: submerge end in sterile water immediately
  • If tube pulls out of chest: cover with petroleum gauze, tape 3 sides

A – O – V – C – T

The respiratory NCLEX framework — apply to every respiratory question

  • Airway — Is it open? Stridor, secretions, obstruction? Act first. Position, suction, call for help.
  • Oxygenation — Is O₂ getting in? Apply appropriate O₂ device. Target SpO₂ ≥95% (88–92% for COPD). Assess breath sounds.
  • Ventilation — Is CO₂ getting out? Assess RR, depth, use of accessory muscles. Check ABGs. Prepare for vent support if needed.
  • Circulation — Is blood perfusing? HR, BP, skin color/temp, cap refill, UO. Hypotension + respiratory distress = critical.
  • Treat the Cause — Bronchospasm → bronchodilator. Infection → antibiotics (culture first). PE → anticoagulation. Pneumothorax → chest tube. Fluid overload → diuretics.
Report IMMEDIATELY (Any of These):
Stridor · Silent chest · Absent breath sounds · SpO₂ falling despite O₂ · Confusion/restlessness (↑CO₂ building) · Tracheal deviation (tension pneumothorax) · New/worsening dyspnea · Cyanosis
Isolation Precautions
🔴 TB — Airborne (N95, negative pressure room)
🟡 Influenza — Droplet (surgical mask within 3–6 ft)
🟢 RSV — Contact (gloves, gown)
🔵 COVID-19 — Airborne/Droplet/Contact (follow facility policy)

6 · Memory Tricks

Mnemonic

ROME — ABG Interpretation

Respiratory = Opposite (pH and CO₂ go opposite directions). Metabolic = Equal (pH and HCO₃⁻ go same direction).

Mnemonic

PE = 3 P's

Pain (pleuritic) · Panic (anxiety/restlessness) · Pulmonary clot (hemoptysis, sudden SOB, tachycardia). Think PE when SUDDEN + UNEXPLAINED.

Trick

Simple Mask = 6 Minimum

Never below 6 L/min — CO₂ accumulates in the mask and patient rebreathes it. If you need <6 L/min, switch to nasal cannula.

Trick

Chest Tube: Never Clamp, Never Raise

Clamping → tension pneumothorax. Raising → fluid drains back into chest. Keep it below, keep the seal, and never silence an alarm without finding the cause.

Trick

COPD O₂ Target = 88–92%

COPD patients breathe off hypoxic drive. Giving too much O₂ can blunt the drive to breathe → respiratory failure. Use Venturi mask for precise control. Target 88–92%, NOT 100%.

Trick

Silent Chest = Worst Asthma

No wheeze sounds better — but in asthma it means no air is moving at all. Silent chest = status asthmaticus = emergency. Call for help immediately.

Trick

High Vent Alarm = Too Much Resistance

Think: something is BLOCKING the airflow (secretions, kink, bronchospasm, patient biting). Low vent alarm = air escaping somewhere (disconnection, leak, extubation).

Trick

Confusion Before Cyanosis

Restlessness and confusion (↑CO₂ or ↓O₂ affecting the brain) come BEFORE visible cyanosis. Never wait for blue lips to act — act when you see confusion in a respiratory patient.

7 · NCLEX Pearls

  • ✅ Cyanosis is a LATE sign of hypoxia — act when you see restlessness and confusion, not when you see blue lips.
  • ✅ COPD does NOT receive unlimited oxygen — target SpO₂ 88–92% using a Venturi mask for precise FiO₂.
  • ✅ Crackles do NOT mean asthma — crackles = fluid (HF, pneumonia, ARDS). Asthma = wheezes.
  • ✅ Stridor = upper airway obstruction, NOT wheezing — act immediately (anaphylaxis, croup, foreign body).
  • ✅ Never clamp a chest tube without a specific physician order and clinical reason — clamping causes tension pneumothorax.
  • ✅ ABGs must be interpreted in context — a COPD patient with CO₂ of 55 may be at their baseline (compensated respiratory acidosis).
  • ✅ PE first sign may be tachycardia only — don't wait for the classic triad. Think PE with any sudden unexplained dyspnea.
  • ✅ Obtain sputum culture BEFORE giving the first antibiotic dose in pneumonia.
  • ✅ Suction only as needed, not routinely — preoxygenate first, limit to 10–15 seconds, hyperoxgenate after.
  • ✅ Venturi mask = the COPD nurse's best friend — delivers precise, controlled FiO₂ without risk of over-oxygenation.
  • ✅ Tracheal deviation = tension pneumothorax — trachea deviates AWAY from the affected side. This is an emergency.
  • ✅ ARDS = refractory hypoxia (O₂ doesn't fix it) + bilateral infiltrates on CXR + noncardiogenic cause.

8 · NCLEX Traps

  • 🚫 Trap: Waiting for cyanosis to intervene. Cyanosis is LATE — act on restlessness, confusion, and rising RR first.
  • 🚫 Trap: Giving a COPD patient a non-rebreather mask at 15 L/min. Can suppress hypoxic drive → apnea. Use Venturi mask, target 88–92%.
  • 🚫 Trap: Thinking crackles = asthma. Crackles = fluid in alveoli. Asthma = wheezes from bronchospasm.
  • 🚫 Trap: Giving antibiotics before obtaining a sputum culture. Culture first — always.
  • 🚫 Trap: Clamping a chest tube when the patient stands up or is moving. This is dangerous and not standard practice.
  • 🚫 Trap: Silencing a vent alarm and continuing care. Always find and fix the cause before silencing.
  • 🚫 Trap: Missing PE because there's no chest pain. PE can present as only tachycardia, anxiety, or sudden SOB.
  • 🚫 Trap: Thinking a silent chest in asthma means improvement. Silent = no airflow = status asthmaticus = emergency.
  • 🚫 Trap: Interpreting a "normal" CO₂ in a COPD patient as reassuring. Their baseline may be 55 — a "normal" 40 could mean they're tiring out.
  • 🚫 Trap: Assuming Theophylline is safe — it has a narrow therapeutic index. Monitor levels; toxicity causes seizures and arrhythmias.

9 · Clinical Connections

🫁 COPD Exacerbation Patient

Increased dyspnea, purulent sputum, ↑CO₂. Priority: Venturi mask O₂ (88–92%), bronchodilators (albuterol + ipratropium), oral/IV steroids, antibiotics if infection suspected. Assess for respiratory fatigue — rising CO₂ with worsening LOC = may need intubation.

🏥 Post-Op Patient (Atelectasis Risk)

After surgery, splinting from pain → shallow breathing → atelectasis. Incentive spirometer q1h while awake, deep breathing, early ambulation, adequate pain control (so patient CAN take deep breaths), positioning HOB 30–45°.

🩸 Patient with Suspected PE

Post-op day 3, sudden SOB + tachycardia + pleuritic chest pain + leg swelling. Priority: O₂, call provider, anticipate CT-PA order, heparin drip. Maintain bed rest (to prevent clot dislodgment). DVT prophylaxis is prevention: SCDs, LMWH, early ambulation.

🤖 Mechanically Ventilated Patient

High-pressure alarm: check for secretions (suction), kinks in tubing, patient biting (bite block), or bronchospasm (give bronchodilator). Low-pressure: check all connections, look for extubation. Document and report all alarm events. Never restrain without order.

🦠 Pneumonia Patient

Priority positioning: HOB 30–45° (semi-Fowler's) to facilitate lung expansion and prevent aspiration. Encourage fluids (unless restricted) — thins secretions. Encourage coughing and deep breathing. Monitor temp, WBC, sputum character, and O₂ sat trends.

🔴 TB Patient

Airborne precautions: N95 respirator (not surgical mask) for all staff entering room. Negative pressure room. Patient wears surgical mask when transported. Treatment: RIPE therapy (Rifampin, Isoniazid, Pyrazinamide, Ethambutol) × 2 months, then continue INH + Rifampin × 4 months.

10 · Priority Framework

🫁 Respiratory NCLEX Priority Order

  1. Is the airway open? Stridor, secretions, obstruction → act immediately (suction, position, call for help)
  2. Is oxygenation adequate? SpO₂ <90% (or <88% COPD) → apply O₂, select correct device
  3. Is ventilation occurring? RR <12 or >20, CO₂ rising → prepare for escalated support
  4. Is the patient perfusing? BP, HR, cap refill, LOC, UO → signs of shock = critical
  5. Treat the cause → cultures, antibiotics, anticoagulation, bronchodilators, chest tube as appropriate
Call Provider Immediately
• Stridor (upper airway obstruction)
• Silent chest in asthma (status asthmaticus)
• SpO₂ <90% not responding to O₂
• Tracheal deviation (tension pneumothorax)
• RR <12 on opioids (give naloxone)
• Sudden severe dyspnea (think PE)
• Absent breath sounds unilaterally
Which Patient Is Priority? (NCLEX)
Always pick: actual problem over potential, airway over everything else.

Patient A: SpO₂ 86%, RR 28, use of accessory muscles
Patient B: SpO₂ 94%, mild wheeze, RR 18
Patient C: SpO₂ 97%, asking for pain medication

→ Patient A is priority every time.

11 · Medication Focus

Albuterol (ProAir, Ventolin)

Short-Acting Beta-2 Agonist (SABA) · Rescue Inhaler
Action:Beta-2 agonist → bronchodilation (fast-acting relief)
Uses:Acute bronchospasm in asthma and COPD; exercise-induced bronchoconstriction
Onset:5–15 minutes; duration 4–6 hours
Side Effects:Tachycardia, tremors, ↓ potassium (hypokalemia), palpitations, nervousness
Teach:Rescue inhaler — use first during attack; rinse mouth after; not for daily maintenance
⚠️ Monitor HR (tachycardia) · Monitor K+ (hypokalemia) · SABA = relief; not prevention

Ipratropium (Atrovent)

Anticholinergic Bronchodilator
Action:Blocks muscarinic receptors → bronchodilation + ↓ secretions
Uses:COPD maintenance; used with albuterol (Combivent) for acute exacerbations
Side Effects:Dry mouth, urinary retention, constipation, blurred vision, tachycardia
Teach:Rinse mouth after use; not a rescue inhaler (slower onset than albuterol); good for COPD maintenance
⚠️ Caution in BPH (urinary retention) and glaucoma · Dry mouth is common

Fluticasone (Flovent)

Inhaled Corticosteroid (ICS) · Maintenance
Action:Anti-inflammatory → reduces airway inflammation (prevents attacks)
Uses:Asthma maintenance; COPD with frequent exacerbations
Side Effects:Oral candidiasis (thrush), hoarseness, adrenal suppression (long-term high dose)
Teach:Rinse mouth and gargle with water after EVERY use (prevents thrush); not a rescue inhaler — takes days to weeks to work
⚠️ Rinse mouth after use — oral thrush risk · NOT for acute bronchospasm

Theophylline

Methylxanthine Bronchodilator
Action:Bronchodilator + mild anti-inflammatory; inhibits phosphodiesterase
Uses:COPD, refractory asthma — older drug, less commonly used now
Therapeutic:10–20 mcg/mL — NARROW therapeutic window
Toxicity Signs:Nausea/vomiting, tachycardia, tremors, seizures, arrhythmias
Interactions:Caffeine ↑ levels; smoking ↓ levels; many drug interactions (erythromycin ↑ levels)
⚠️ Narrow therapeutic range — monitor levels · Toxicity = seizures + arrhythmias

13 · NGN Case Studies

📋 Case 1: "He's Breathing Faster and Getting Confused"

▼
Mr. Davis, 72 years old, has a 45-pack-year smoking history and known COPD. He was admitted for a COPD exacerbation. Current vitals: RR 26, HR 104, SpO₂ 84% on room air, BP 138/86, Temp 38.2°C. He is using accessory muscles, has pursed-lip breathing, and is becoming increasingly restless and confused. His baseline SpO₂ is 90–91%. ABG drawn: pH 7.30, PaCO₂ 62, HCO₃⁻ 29.
1. Interpret the ABG and explain why it makes clinical sense for this patient.
pH 7.30 = acidosis. PaCO₂ 62 (↑) = respiratory cause (ROME: Respiratory = Opposite, pH↓ CO₂↑). HCO₃⁻ 29 (↑) = kidneys are compensating by retaining bicarbonate. This is partially compensated respiratory acidosis. Clinical sense: COPD patient is in exacerbation → can't exhale CO₂ → CO₂ builds up → acidosis. The kidneys have partially compensated but pH is still acidotic — the patient is not fully compensated, meaning this is an acute-on-chronic deterioration.
2. The nurse applies a non-rebreather mask at 12 L/min. SpO₂ rises to 98%. Thirty minutes later, Mr. Davis is less responsive. What happened?
The nurse over-oxygenated the COPD patient. COPD patients with chronic CO₂ retention lose their central chemoreceptor sensitivity to CO₂ — they breathe primarily off the hypoxic drive (low O₂ stimulates breathing). Giving high-flow O₂ raises SpO₂ → removes the hypoxic drive → the patient stops breathing effectively → CO₂ rises → hypercapnic narcosis (CO₂ narcosis) → decreased LOC. Correct O₂ target: SpO₂ 88–92% using a Venturi mask for precise FiO₂ control.
3. What is the correct oxygen management for Mr. Davis?
Switch to a Venturi mask at 24–28% FiO₂, titrating to maintain SpO₂ 88–92%. Reassess frequently. Add bronchodilators (albuterol + ipratropium nebulizers), systemic corticosteroids (prednisone or methylprednisolone), and antibiotics if infection suspected. If he continues to deteriorate (rising CO₂, worsening LOC, RR falling) → non-invasive positive pressure ventilation (BiPAP) → intubation as last resort.
4. What are the priority assessments and teaching points before discharge?
Before discharge: confirm SpO₂ at baseline on prescribed home O₂ (if applicable), verify inhaler technique (rescue SABA + maintenance ICS/LABA), teach pursed-lip breathing and tripod positioning to reduce air trapping. Teaching: smoking cessation (if still smoking), flu and pneumonia vaccines, avoid respiratory irritants, call provider for increased dyspnea or sputum changes, use albuterol FIRST if both inhalers are prescribed.

📋 Case 2: "She Just Had Her Hip Replaced — Why Is She So Anxious?"

▼
Ms. Chen, 58 years old, is post-operative day 2 following a left total hip replacement. She has been on bed rest and had sequential compression devices (SCDs) removed for a dressing change 2 hours ago. She calls the nurse saying she feels "very anxious for no reason" and is short of breath. Vitals: HR 118, RR 24, BP 106/72, SpO₂ 91% on room air. She has no fever. She denies productive cough. She has mild left calf swelling.
1. What does this clinical picture suggest and what is the priority nursing action?
Pulmonary embolism (PE). Classic presentation: post-op patient (high DVT risk), sudden onset anxiety + dyspnea + tachycardia + hypoxia + calf swelling (DVT source). SUDDEN + UNEXPLAINED = think PE. Priority: apply O₂ immediately (high flow), call the provider STAT, maintain bed rest (don't ambulate — risk of dislodging clot), continuous cardiac monitoring, establish IV access, anticipate orders for CT pulmonary angiography.
2. What ABG finding would you expect in the early stage of PE and why?
Early PE → patient hyperventilates from anxiety and hypoxia → blows off CO₂ → respiratory alkalosis: pH >7.45, PaCO₂ <35, HCO₃⁻ normal. This is counterintuitive — the patient looks terrible but their ABG shows alkalosis, not acidosis. Late/severe PE with significant hypoxia progresses to metabolic and respiratory acidosis as tissues suffer and the patient fatigues.
3. The provider orders a heparin drip. What nursing priorities apply?
Heparin: establish weight-based dosing per protocol. Check baseline aPTT, CBC, and PT/INR before starting. Monitor aPTT q6h (goal 60–100 sec = 1.5–2.5× normal). Assess for bleeding (hematoma at surgical site, bleeding gums, blood in urine/stool, neurological changes). Keep protamine sulfate available (antidote). Do NOT give IM injections. Monitor platelet count daily (HIT risk after 5–10 days).
4. How could this PE have been prevented?
DVT/PE prevention (Virchow's Triad targets):
• Stasis: early ambulation post-op, SCDs while in bed (should NOT have been removed without immediate replacement), leg exercises
• Hypercoagulability: pharmacologic prophylaxis — LMWH (enoxaparin) or fondaparinux as ordered
• Vessel damage: surgical — can't prevent, but adequate hydration and avoiding dehydration helps
The SCDs being removed without replacement for 2 hours was a contributing factor in this case.
