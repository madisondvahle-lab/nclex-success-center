Phase 1 · Foundations

1 · Module Overview

Why this module matters: Pharmacology questions appear on every NCLEX — they're woven into med-surg, maternal, pediatric, and psych questions alike. You won't be asked to memorize every drug, but you MUST understand drug classes, mechanisms, key adverse effects, and nursing priorities.

🎯 What You'll Master

  • Pharmacokinetics (ADME) in plain English
  • High-alert medications and why they're deadly
  • Top drug classes: anticoagulants, cardiac, antibiotics, psych, pain
  • Safe medication administration & the 10 Rights
  • Key antidotes and reversal agents
  • Priority nursing assessments before/after giving drugs

📊 NCLEX Weight

Pharmacological and Parenteral Therapies = ~13–19% of NCLEX-RN questions (the single largest subcategory in Safe and Effective Care).

Pro Tip Know the drug class → you can reason about any drug in it. NCLEX loves new drug names you've never seen — if you know the class suffix, you know the mechanism.

🫁 Absorption factors

  • Route: IV = fastest; oral = slowest onset
  • First-pass effect: oral drugs metabolized in liver before systemic circulation (reduces bioavailability)
  • Food interactions can ↑ or ↓ absorption

🩸 Distribution factors

  • Protein binding: highly bound drugs stay in blood longer
  • Blood-brain barrier: only lipid-soluble drugs cross
  • Volume of distribution (Vd): large Vd = stored in tissues

🏥 Metabolism (Liver)

  • Cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYP450) — site of most interactions
  • Liver failure → drug toxicity risk
  • Prodrugs: metabolized INTO active form (codeine → morphine)

🫘 Excretion (Kidneys)

  • Most drugs excreted renally → renal failure = drug accumulation
  • Watch creatinine/GFR before renally cleared drugs
  • Some drugs excreted in bile (biliary) → feces

🚫 Never Crush / Break / Chew List

Crushing these medications causes dose dumping, GI damage, or destroys the time-release mechanism:

ER · Extended Release XR · Extended Release XL · Extended Release SR · Sustained Release CR · Controlled Release LA · Long Acting EC · Enteric Coated
NCLEX Application If a patient has a feeding tube or difficulty swallowing, always check if the tablet can be crushed BEFORE giving it. If it cannot → contact the provider for an alternative formulation (liquid, patch, different drug).

🧮 Drug Calculation Conversions (Must Memorize)

Essential Conversions

  • 1 g = 1,000 mg
  • 1 mg = 1,000 mcg
  • 1 mEq = 1 mmol (approx.)
  • 1 mL = 1 cc
  • 1 unit insulin = 1 mL in U-100 syringe
  • mcg = microgram · mg = milligram · g = gram
  • mL = milliliter · mEq = milliequivalent

Key Medication Vocabulary

  • Loading dose: Higher initial dose to reach therapeutic level fast
  • Maintenance dose: Ongoing dose to maintain therapeutic effect
  • Peak: Highest drug concentration in blood
  • Trough: Lowest drug concentration (drawn just before next dose)
  • Half-life: Time for drug level to decrease by 50%
  • Steady state: Drug intake = elimination; level is stable (4–5 half-lives)
  • Therapeutic index: Difference between toxic and therapeutic levels

5 · Deep Dive Lessons

Core Concept Pharmacokinetics = what the BODY does to the DRUG. Every step has NCLEX implications.
PhaseWhat HappensClinical RelevanceNCLEX Watch
AbsorptionDrug enters bloodstream from administration siteIV bypasses absorption → immediate effect; oral has variable absorptionIV = fastest onset; sublingual next; oral = slowest
DistributionDrug moves from blood to tissuesLow albumin → more free drug → toxicity risk (elderly, malnourished)Protein-bound drugs compete → drug interactions
MetabolismLiver transforms drug (mainly CYP450)Liver disease → reduced metabolism → drug accumulatesCheck LFTs before hepatically metabolized drugs
ExcretionKidneys remove drug from bodyRenal failure → drug accumulates → toxicityCheck BUN/Cr before renally cleared drugs
First-Pass Effect Oral drugs absorbed from GI → go straight to liver via portal circulation → partially metabolized BEFORE reaching systemic circulation. That's why oral morphine dose = much higher than IV dose.
Half-Life Time for plasma concentration to decrease by 50%. Drug reaches steady state after 4–5 half-lives. Drugs with long half-lives accumulate in renal/hepatic impairment → toxicity.

⚗️ Pharmacodynamics in 60 Seconds

  • Agonist: binds receptor → activates it (mimics normal signal) — ex: morphine is opioid agonist
  • Antagonist: binds receptor → BLOCKS it (blocks normal signal) — ex: naloxone blocks opioid receptors
  • Partial agonist: binds and partially activates — ex: buprenorphine
  • Therapeutic index: ratio of toxic dose to therapeutic dose; narrow = high risk (warfarin, digoxin, lithium, aminoglycosides)
  • Tolerance: need more drug for same effect (opioids, benzos)
  • Tachyphylaxis: rapid tolerance after repeated doses

🩸 HIT — Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia

  • Platelets drop >50% within 5–10 days of heparin exposure
  • Paradoxical CLOTTING (not bleeding) — white clot syndrome
  • Stop ALL heparin (including flushes, LMWH)
  • Switch to argatroban or bivalirudin (direct thrombin inhibitors)
  • Do NOT give platelets — worsens clotting

🔄 3 Checks Before Giving Any Drug

  1. Check label when removing from storage
  2. Check label when preparing/drawing up
  3. Check label before administering to patient
Never document before giving the drug — document immediately after administration.

💉 IV Compatibility

  • Always check compatibility before mixing/co-infusing
  • Flush line before and after each medication with incompatible drugs
  • Phenytoin: compatible ONLY with NS (not D5W — precipitates)
  • Amphotericin B: lipid formulations preferred to reduce nephrotoxicity
  • Blood products: ONLY NS (Lactated Ringer's can cause hemolysis)

🔴 When to Hold a Drug (NCLEX)

  • Digoxin: HR <60 or K+ abnormal
  • Any beta blocker: HR <60 or SBP <90
  • Any antihypertensive: SBP <90
  • Opioid: RR <12 or sedation score too high
  • Insulin: blood glucose too low for meal not ready
  • Metformin: hold 48h before/after contrast dye
  • Anticoagulant: active bleeding or critically low platelets

6 · Memory Tricks

Mnemonic

ADME = "A Drug Makes Everything"

Absorption → Distribution → Metabolism → Excretion. Remember: the body processes every drug in this order.

Mnemonic

HIGH-ALERT = "Wild Inmates Like Morphine Periodically Killing"

Warfarin · Insulin · Lithium · Morphine/opioids · Potassium (IV) · Kemo drugs · Digoxin

Mnemonic

Digoxin Toxicity = "Yellow HAB"

Yellow-green halos (visual) · Heart block/bradycardia · Anorexia/nausea/vomiting · Bigeminy (irregular rhythm)

Mnemonic

NMS vs. Serotonin Syndrome

NMS: antipsychotics, SLOW onset (days), lead-pipe rigidity, no clonus. Serotonin: serotonergic drugs, FAST onset (<24h), hyperreflexia + clonus. Both → stop the drug.

Trick

Warfarin: "I KAVe War"

Warfarin inhibits clotting factors that need Vitamin K: factors II, VII, IX, X. Antidote = Vitamin K. INR >3 → bleeding risk.

Trick

-mycin Toxicities: "ENT"

Aminoglycosides hurt the Ears (ototoxic) and Nephrons (nephrotoxic). Monitor for Tough trough levels. Always check BUN/Cr before giving.

Trick

ACE Inhibitors: "CAPTOPRIL Side Effects"

Cough · Angioedema (STOP drug) · Potassium ↑ · Teratogenic · Orthostatic hypotension (first dose) · Proteinuria ↓ · Renal protection · Increase creatinine initially · Low BP

Trick

Lithium Toxicity: "DATT"

Diarrhea/nausea · Ataxia (tremors, shaking) · Toxicity from low sodium (Na+ out → Li+ in) · Transient memory issues → progresses to seizures/coma

7 · NCLEX Pearls

  • ✅ Always obtain cultures BEFORE starting antibiotics — then start empiric therapy. Don't wait for results.
  • ✅ Digoxin: hold if apical pulse <60 OR if K+ is low (hypokalemia potentiates toxicity).
  • ✅ ACE inhibitors cause a persistent dry cough due to bradykinin accumulation — switch to ARB if intolerable.
  • ✅ Angioedema from ACE inhibitors = STOP drug immediately — it's a medical emergency (airway compromise).
  • ✅ Metformin: hold 48 hours before AND after IV contrast dye (risk of lactic acidosis in renal impairment).
  • ✅ Warfarin: many drug interactions (antibiotics ↑ INR, vitamin K foods ↓ INR). Consistent diet, not elimination.
  • ✅ Statins + muscle pain = stop the drug and check CK level (rhabdomyolysis can cause acute kidney injury).
  • ✅ SSRIs take 2–4 weeks to work — monitor closely for suicidal ideation in the FIRST 2 weeks as energy returns before mood lifts.
  • ✅ Clozapine (Clozaril) requires weekly WBC monitoring for agranulocytosis — if ANC <1000, stop the drug.
  • ✅ Fentanyl patch: avoid heat (heating pads, fever) — increases absorption and can cause overdose.
  • ✅ Spironolactone + ACE inhibitor = dangerous hyperkalemia. Never give K+ supplements concurrently without close monitoring.
  • ✅ Vancomycin Red Man Syndrome: slow the infusion rate and pretreat with diphenhydramine — it's NOT an allergy.

8 · NCLEX Traps

  • 🚫 Trap: "Give the antibiotic, then get the cultures." WRONG — cultures FIRST, then antibiotics.
  • 🚫 Trap: Thinking Red Man Syndrome = allergy. It's rate-related — slow down, don't stop forever.
  • 🚫 Trap: Stopping beta-blockers abruptly in a hospitalized patient. ALWAYS taper — abrupt stop = rebound tachycardia/angina.
  • 🚫 Trap: Treating opioid-induced GI upset with anti-emetics and expecting it to fix constipation. Constipation needs its own bowel regimen — always.
  • 🚫 Trap: INR 2.8 on warfarin = therapeutic if indicated for DVT/PE. Don't automatically hold — check the indication.
  • 🚫 Trap: Cephalosporin in a penicillin-allergic patient. Cross-reactivity exists (~10%) — check allergy type (anaphylaxis vs. rash).
  • 🚫 Trap: Giving lithium to a patient on a low-sodium diet. Low Na = more lithium reabsorbed = toxicity. Na and hydration MUST be adequate.
  • 🚫 Trap: Giving a fluoroquinolone to a patient with a history of tendon issues or in a pediatric patient. These are contraindications.
  • 🚫 Trap: Documenting a medication BEFORE you give it. ALWAYS administer first, then document.
  • 🚫 Trap: Assuming the oral and IV doses of opioids are equivalent. Oral morphine 30mg ≈ IV morphine 10mg (first-pass effect).

9 · Clinical Connections

🏥 Renal Failure Patient

All renally cleared drugs accumulate → toxicity. Check BUN/Cr before: aminoglycosides, vancomycin, metformin, digoxin, gabapentin, methotrexate. Dose must be adjusted or drug held.

🫀 Heart Failure Patient

NSAIDs worsen HF (retain Na/fluid). CCBs (verapamil/diltiazem) decrease contractility → avoid in systolic HF. Loop diuretics → hypokalemia → digoxin toxicity risk. Monitor K+ closely.

🍸 Alcohol Use Disorder

Metronidazole + alcohol = severe disulfiram reaction (flushing, vomiting, palpitations). Acetaminophen max 2g/day (or avoid). MAOIs + alcohol = hypertensive crisis risk.

👵 Elderly Patient

Decreased renal/hepatic function → drugs stay longer → lower doses needed. High-risk: benzos (falls), anticholinergics (confusion), long-acting opioids. Beers Criteria lists drugs to avoid.

🤰 Pregnant Patient

Avoid: tetracyclines (teeth/bone), fluoroquinolones, ACE inhibitors (fetal renal anomalies), NSAIDs in 3rd trimester (premature ductus closure), live vaccines, warfarin (category X).

🔬 Lab Values to Know

  • INR 2–3 = therapeutic (most); 2.5–3.5 = mechanical valves
  • aPTT 60–100 sec = therapeutic heparin
  • Digoxin 0.5–2 ng/mL (therapeutic)
  • Lithium 0.6–1.2 mEq/L (maintenance)
  • Vancomycin trough 10–20 mcg/mL
  • Phenytoin 10–20 mcg/mL

10 · Priority Framework

💊 Before Giving ANY Drug — Ask Yourself:

  1. Is there a HOLD parameter? (HR, BP, RR, blood glucose, lab values)
  2. Does the patient have allergies? (check MAR; 2 patient identifiers)
  3. Is the dose safe? (calculate; verify with pharmacy if unsure)
  4. Is the route appropriate? (can patient swallow? IV access patent?)
  5. Any drug interactions? (especially high-alert meds)
  6. Any relevant labs? (renal/hepatic function for clearance)
ALWAYS Intervene Immediately
• Angioedema from ACE inhibitor (airway)
• RR <12 on opioids (give naloxone)
• Signs of anaphylaxis (epinephrine + stop drug)
• NMS or serotonin syndrome (stop offending agent)
• Signs of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (stop all heparin)
Notify Provider — Do Not Hold Without Order
• Digoxin: HR <60 → hold + notify
• New drug allergic reaction → stop + notify
• INR supratherapeutic (warfarin) → hold + notify
• Lithium toxicity signs → hold + notify
• Patient refuses medication → document + notify

11 · Medication Focus

Warfarin (Coumadin)

Anticoagulant · Vitamin K Antagonist
Action:Inhibits Vitamin K-dependent clotting factors (II, VII, IX, X)
Uses:DVT/PE treatment/prevention; A-fib; mechanical heart valves
Monitor:INR (therapeutic 2–3; mechanical valves 2.5–3.5)
Antidote:Vitamin K (slow); FFP or 4-factor PCC (fast)
Teach:Consistent green vegetable intake (don't eliminate); avoid NSAIDs; report any unusual bleeding; many drug interactions (antibiotics ↑ INR)
⚠️ NARROW therapeutic window · Onset 24–72 hours · Teratogenic (Category X)

Regular Insulin (Humulin R)

Antidiabetic · Short-Acting Insulin
Onset:30–60 min; peak 2–4 hr; duration 5–8 hr
Uses:Type 1 & 2 diabetes; DKA; hyperkalemia (with dextrose)
Before:Check glucose; ensure meal ready; verify type, dose, route with 2nd RN
Antidote:D50W (IV); glucagon (IM/SC if no IV access); D10W (infusion)
Teach:Rotate injection sites; store opened insulin at room temp; signs of hypoglycemia (shakiness, sweating, confusion)
⚠️ HIGH-ALERT · Must have 2-nurse verification · Hypoglycemia risk

Morphine Sulfate

Opioid Analgesic · Mu-Agonist
Action:Binds mu-opioid receptors → analgesia, sedation, euphoria, resp. depression
Uses:Moderate-to-severe pain; acute pulmonary edema; MI pain
Before:RR, sedation level, BP, bowel function; have naloxone available
Antidote:Naloxone (Narcan) — 0.4–2 mg IV/IM/SC/IN; repeat q2–3 min
Teach:Do not drive; take with food (nausea); always on a bowel regimen; report RR <12, excessive drowsiness

Metformin (Glucophage)

Biguanide · Antidiabetic
Action:↓ hepatic glucose production; ↑ insulin sensitivity; does NOT cause hypoglycemia alone
Uses:Type 2 diabetes (first-line); PCOS
Hold When:IV contrast dye (hold 48h before/after); GFR <30; active HF; major surgery; acute illness
Monitor:Renal function (BUN/Cr), B12 levels (long-term use depletes B12)

📋 Case 1: The Patient Who's "Too Tired" After Morphine

▼
Mr. Rodriguez, 68 years old, is post-operative day 1 following a left hip replacement. He has a history of COPD and mild renal insufficiency (Cr 1.8). He is prescribed morphine sulfate 4 mg IV q4h PRN for pain (rated 7/10). At 1400, the nurse assesses him: RR 10/min, SpO₂ 89% on 2L O₂ NC (baseline 94%), HR 58, BP 102/64. The patient is drowsy but can be aroused. His last morphine dose was at 1200 (2 hours ago).
1. What is the nurse's PRIORITY assessment finding?
Respiratory rate of 10/min (below 12/min threshold) with SpO₂ of 89% — signs of opioid-induced respiratory depression. This is the priority life-threatening concern. COPD already compromises respiratory reserve, making this patient especially vulnerable.
2. What immediate action should the nurse take?
Administer naloxone (Narcan) per PRN order (typically 0.4–2 mg IV). Stay with the patient. Apply oxygen/increase O₂ as needed. Notify the provider. Document the event. Note: naloxone's half-life is shorter than morphine's — monitor for return of respiratory depression and repeat doses may be needed.
3. How do Mr. Rodriguez's co-morbidities (COPD + renal insufficiency) affect his pharmacokinetic response to morphine?
COPD: already-compromised respiratory drive means morphine's respiratory depression effect is amplified at lower doses.

Renal insufficiency: morphine's active metabolite (morphine-6-glucuronide) is renally excreted. With reduced GFR, this active metabolite accumulates → prolonged and enhanced opioid effect even with standard dosing. The nurse should flag these risk factors and anticipate the provider may want to adjust the dose or switch to a different analgesic.
4. What teaching will the nurse provide when Mr. Rodriguez is alert and ready for discharge with oral opioids?
Key discharge teaching for opioids:
• Do not drive or operate machinery
• Avoid alcohol and other CNS depressants
• Take with food to reduce nausea
• A bowel regimen (stool softener + laxative) is mandatory — constipation will NOT improve on its own
• Do not take more than prescribed or use more frequently
• Store safely, away from children (medication lockbox recommended)
• Have naloxone (Narcan) at home; ensure a family member knows how to use it
• Report persistent RR <12, extreme drowsiness, inability to wake to 911

📋 Case 2: Anticoagulation Gone Wrong

▼
Mrs. Thompson, 72 years old, was admitted with atrial fibrillation and started on IV heparin followed by oral warfarin. She is now on day 9 of heparin. This morning's labs: Platelets 61,000/mm³ (admission platelet count was 285,000). INR 2.6 (therapeutic range 2–3). The patient is complaining of right leg pain and swelling — her left leg was the previously affected limb for a prior DVT. She has no active bleeding symptoms.
1. What complication does this clinical picture suggest, and what is the pathophysiology?
Heparin-Induced Thrombocytopenia (HIT) — Type II. After 5–10 days of heparin exposure, the immune system creates antibodies against heparin-platelet factor 4 complexes. These antibodies activate platelets, causing platelet aggregation (thrombosis) and platelet consumption (thrombocytopenia). Paradoxically: platelets fall BUT the patient clots — "white clot syndrome." The new right leg DVT (opposite to her known affected limb) on heparin is the hallmark sign.
2. What is the immediate nursing action?
Stop ALL heparin products immediately — this includes IV heparin, LMWH (enoxaparin), heparin flushes, and heparin-coated catheters. Notify the provider STAT. Do NOT transfuse platelets — this will worsen thrombosis ("feed the clot"). The provider will order an alternative anticoagulant (direct thrombin inhibitor: argatroban or bivalirudin). Warfarin should NOT be the only anticoagulant used during acute HIT (risk of venous limb gangrene).
3. The provider orders the 4Ts score to be calculated. What four components make up this scoring tool?
The 4Ts HIT probability score:
• Thrombocytopenia: degree of platelet fall (>50% drop = 2 pts)
• Timing: days 5–10 of heparin or ≤1 day if prior heparin within 30 days (2 pts)
• Thrombosis: new thrombosis or skin necrosis (2 pts)
• oTher causes: no other explanation for thrombocytopenia (2 pts)
Score 6–8 = high probability of HIT → send anti-PF4/heparin antibody test; stop heparin and start alternative.
4. Mrs. Thompson's INR is therapeutic on warfarin. Can warfarin alone be continued?
No. Warfarin should NOT be used as the sole anticoagulant during active HIT. Warfarin depletes Protein C and S (natural anticoagulants) faster than clotting factors, creating a transient hypercoagulable state that can cause venous limb gangrene. A direct thrombin inhibitor (argatroban) must be bridged with warfarin until the INR is therapeutic on warfarin alone AND platelets have recovered (typically >150,000). Then warfarin can continue as monotherapy.
