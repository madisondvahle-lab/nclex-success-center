NUR 321 · Exam 2 Study Guide

HIGH-RISK PREGNANCY

Anatomy scan — 1 point

A detailed ultrasound, usually around 18–22 weeks, checks fetal anatomy for structural abnormalities.

DON’T FALL FOR THIS: An anatomy scan looks at structure; it is not the same as a BPP, which evaluates fetal well-being.

Identifying a high-risk pregnancy (4 categories) — 1 point

Biophysical: Genetic conditions; poor nutrition, low/high BMI or food insecurity; chronic illness such as hypertension, hyperglycemia, asthma, thyroid dysfunction, STIs or seizure disorders; previous pregnancy loss, fibroids, uterine surgery or multiple gestation.

Psychosocial: Tobacco/substance use and mental health conditions. Screen for perinatal anxiety and depression.

Sociodemographic: Adolescent pregnancy, maternal age over 35, parity, social determinants of health and access to care.

Environmental: Lead exposure, medications and relevant animal, food or activity exposure

Polyhydramnios (too much amniotic fluid)— 1 point

Polyhydramnios is listed as a condition that may require increased fetal testing.

Associated with maternal diabetes, multiple pregnancy, or fetal problems that interfere with swallowing; sometimes the cause is unknown.

Teach daily fetal movement counting: Count movements over two hours or record how long it takes to reach 10 movements.

PRIORITY: Contact the provider for decreased movement.

Fetal Surveillance in high-risk pregnancies typically begins around 32 weeks.

Alpha-fetoprotein test — 1 point

AFP is a maternal blood screening test.

Abnormal levels may suggest fetal trisomy or spina bifida.

DON’T FALL FOR THIS: Screening does not establish a diagnosis—abnormal findings require follow-up testing.

AFP is also one component of the quad screen, along with hCG, estriol and inhibin A. (slides 12-13)

Biophysical profile — 3 points (BPP assesses fetal well-being, not genetic abnormalities.)

Each component receives 0 or 2 points, for a maximum of 10.

8–10 = acceptable

6 = follow-up

4 or below = immediate birth

MUST KNOW: The five components

B – breathing

A – amniotic fluid

T - Tone

M - Movement

A – and

N -NST (non-stress test)

HEMORRHAGIC DISORDERS

Placenta previa — 1 point

Placenta covers the cervical opening; a low-lying placenta is near the opening.

Recognize: Painless vaginal bleeding.

Risk factors: Previous previa, cesarean birth/uterine surgery, multiple gestation.

Evaluation: Ultrasound, CBC, type and screen, coagulation studies.

PRIORITY: Assess maternal bleeding/stability and fetal status.

DO NOT: Perform a digital vaginal examination when previa is suspected.

Placenta previa generally requires cesarean birth.

Ectopic pregnancy — 1 point

The guide specifically emphasizes causes and risk factors.

Implantation outside the uterine cavity, usually in a fallopian tube.

Risk factors: Previous ectopic pregnancy, PID, endometriosis, infertility, smoking, and increasing age.

Pregnancy occurring with an IUD in place warrants evaluation for ectopic pregnancy.

Recognize: Localized abdominal/pelvic pain and bleeding.

Rupture warning: Worsening/generalized pain, fainting, or instability.

Evaluation/treatment: hCG and ultrasound; selected cases receive methotrexate, while others require surgery.

Placental abruption — 2 points

Placenta separates prematurely from the uterine wall.

Recognize: Painful bleeding and a tender, rigid abdomen.

Risk factors: Hypertension, cocaine use, smoking, trauma, previous abruption.

Evaluation: CBC, blood type/Rh, coagulation studies, ultrasound.

PRIORITY: Assess maternal circulation and fetal status; prepare for urgent birth when compromised. Mild cases may be monitored.

HIGH-YIELD COMPARISON:Previa = painless bleeding + no digital vaginal exam.Abruption = painful bleeding + rigid abdomen.

Course source: Hemorrhagic Disorders, slides 14–19.

HYPERTENSIVE DISORDERS

Magnesium sulfate — 1 point

Used for seizure prevention/treatment in preeclampsia/eclampsia.

MUST KNOW: Monitor respiratory rate, SpO₂, deep tendon reflexes, urine output, and level of consciousness hourly.

Toxicity: Decreased/absent reflexes, respiratory depression; severe toxicity can cause cardiac arrest.

PRIORITY/FIRST ACTION: Stop the infusion, obtain urgent assistance, and administer calcium gluconate as ordered.

MEMORY TRICK: Magnesium toxicity → calcium gluconate.

Hypertension — 1 point – 3 types to cover

Chronic hypertension: Present before pregnancy.

Gestational hypertension: Develops during pregnancy.

Preeclampsia: Hypertension after 20 weeks with proteinuria and/or organ dysfunction.

Concerning findings: Severe headache, visual changes, right-upper-quadrant pain, hyperreflexia, low platelets, elevated liver enzymes.

Assessments/labs: BP, urine protein, CBC/platelets, creatinine, liver enzymes.

Meds include labetalol, nifedipine, hydralazine, and methyldopa.

HELLP = Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets.

Course source: Hypertensive Disorders, slides 3–9.

ENDOCRINE DISORDERS

Gestational diabetes — 1 point

Glucose intolerance during pregnancy; pattern resembling type 2 diabetes.

Screening: Usually 24–28 weeks.

1-hour > than 140 to trigger a 3-hour test.

Risks: Macrosomia, birth injury, preterm birth, neonatal hypoglycemia.

Treatment: Dietary management first; insulin is the preferred medication.

DON’T FALL FOR THIS: An abnormal screening test calls for further testing—it is not automatically the diagnosis.

Hyperemesis gravidarum — 3 points

The guide specifically emphasizes etiology and risk factors.

Severe, persistent vomiting causing dehydration, electrolyte disturbances, and weight loss.

Risk factors: Nulliparity (no living/viable births), motion sickness, migraines, personal or family history of hyperemesis.

Labs: CBC and metabolic panel (BMP or CMP)

Treatment: Vitamin B6/doxylamine; other prescribed antiemetics as needed. IV fluids for significant dehydration; nutritional support for severe malnutrition.

Nursing care: Monitor hydration and intake, reduce triggers, encourage small frequent high-carbohydrate/low-fat snacks as tolerated.

DON’T FALL FOR THIS: Hyperemesis is more than ordinary nausea—it affects hydration, electrolytes, and nutrition.

Course source: Endocrine Disorders, slides 3–6.

LABOR

Group B streptococcus — 1 point

Screening swab at 36 weeks 0 days–37 weeks 6 days.

GBS can colonize the mother without illness but cause serious newborn infection.

MUST KNOW: When indicated, IV penicillin during labor until birth reduces transmission risk.

Leopold maneuvers — 1 point (0 slides that go over this)

Four abdominal palpation maneuvers help determine fetal position, presenting part, and the baby’s descent into and out of the pelvis.

How it’s done: Assess what is in the fundus, locate the baby’s back (fetal heart sounds easiest to hear), identify the presenting part (face or occiput), and assess descent/head position.

The head feels firm and round; the back feels smooth; extremities feel irregular.

Fetal heart sounds are generally easiest to hear over the fetal back.

Adaptations and mechanisms of labor — 1 point

Maternal oxygen demand increases; gastric motility decreases.

Contractions temporarily decrease fetal oxygen delivery.

Molding: Fetal skull bones overlap to help passage through the pelvis.

Cardinal movements: Engagement → descent → flexion → internal rotation → extension → external rotation → expulsion.

Fetal lie — 1 point

Longitudinal: Fetal and maternal spines are parallel.

Transverse: Fetal spine lies across the maternal spine.

Oblique: Fetus lies diagonally.

DON’T FALL FOR THIS:Lie = relationship of the spines.Presentation = part entering the pelvis first.Position = fetal landmark’s relationship to the maternal pelvis.

Physiology of labor — 1 point

True labor: Contractions become stronger/closer and produce cervical change.

False labor: Contractions may stop with rest and do not produce progressive cervical change.

5 Ps: Passenger, passageway, powers, position, psyche.

Stages of LABOR

1ST Stage : Labor onset to complete dilation; latent 0–5 cm, active 6–10 cm

2nd stage: Complete dilation to birth.

3rd stage: Birth to delivery of placenta.

MUST KNOW: Dilation = opening; effacement = thinning of cervix; station = descent into pelvis.

FETAL HEART RATE / NONREASSURING PATTERNS — 8 POINTS

Monitor fetal response during labor and complications.

Cord prolapse: May cause variable decelerations or a sudden prolonged deceleration.

Chorioamnionitis: May cause fetal tachycardia along with maternal fever and other infection findings.

Concerning fetal changes require assessment and intervention, not simply continued observation..

Normal baseline HR: 110–160 beats/minute.

Early decelerations: Usually head compression.

Variable decelerations: Usually cord compression.

Late decelerations: Suggest impaired uteroplacental oxygen transfer.

MEMORY TRICK for decelerations:

Early = head; variable = cord; late = placenta.

These associations help explain the cause; management depends on the whole clinical picture.

PRIORITY RESPONSE to concerning changes:

Reposition the patient and assess maternal condition, contractions, and possible causes.

Reduce/stop oxytocin when appropriate; correct maternal hypotension and other contributing problems.

Escalate promptly; persistent, serious abnormalities may require expedited birth.

PAIN MANAGEMENT

Coping — 1 point

Coping well: Can relax between contractions, uses rhythmic breathing/movement, reports coping.

Not coping: Cannot relax, becomes increasingly agitated, has difficulty concentrating.

Nursing care: Assess the patient’s experience; provide calm support, lower stimulation, reposition, and offer appropriate pain-relief options.

Acupressure — 1 point

Nonpharmacologic pain relief.

Examples include effleurage and counterpressure.

Effleurage = light, rhythmic stroking, often over the abdomen.

Counterpressure = firm, sustained pressure to the back/sacrum.

MEMORY TRICK: Effleurage = feather-light; counterpressure = firm.

Opioids — 1 point

Fentanyl and morphine.

Pain relief can be incomplete.

MUST KNOW: Newborn effects can include respiratory depression, lower APGAR scores, and feeding difficulties.

Distinguish systemic opioids from local anesthetic techniques.

Local analgesia — 1 point

Local perineal infiltration: Anesthetic injected into perineal tissue.

Pudendal block: Regional block for perineal pain.

Lidocaine and bupivacaine.

HIGH-YIELD DISTINCTION: Local/perineal analgesia is different from systemic opioid medication.

BIRTH

Episiotomy — 1 point

Surgical incision in the perineum to enlarge the vaginal opening for birth.

DON’T FALL FOR THIS: It is not recommended routinely—only when necessary. ACOG

COMPLICATIONS OF LABOR AND BIRTH

Umbilical cord prolapse — 1 point

Cord slips below the presenting part or becomes compressed.

Recognize: Visible/palpable cord, variable decelerations, or sudden prolonged deceleration.

PRIORITY/FIRST ACTION: Use a sterile gloved hand to lift the presenting part and relieve cord pressure, reposition, obtain immediate assistance, and prepare for emergency birth.

MUST KNOW: Relieve compression while help and delivery are being arranged.

Uterine rupture — 1 point

Major risk: Previous cesarean birth/uterine scar.

Recognize: Loss of fetal station, abdominal or shoulder pain, change in uterine shape/tone.

PRIORITY: Immediate emergency response; prepare for cesarean birth, IV fluids/blood, and surgical repair or hysterectomy.

Chorioamnionitis — 3 points

Infection involving the amniotic fluid, membranes/placenta, and surrounding intrauterine tissues.

Risk factors: Multiple vaginal examinations, meconium, smoking, alcohol use, and poor hygiene.

Recognize: Maternal fever, uterine tenderness, foul-smelling fluid, maternal/fetal tachycardia, elevated WBC.

Evaluation: CBC, inflammatory markers/cultures as ordered; placental testing may occur after birth.

PRIORITY: Administer ordered antibiotics; monitor maternal vital signs, fetal heart rate, and contractions; notify the neonatal team.

DON’T FALL FOR THIS: Infection may affect both mother and fetus—watch both.

Operative vaginal birth — 1 point

Birth assisted with vacuum or forceps.

Nursing preparation: Explain/support, assist with adequate pain relief, empty the bladder, position as directed, monitor FHR.

Risks: Maternal tissue injury/hemorrhage and newborn injury.

Distinction: Vacuum can cause scalp injury/cephalohematoma; forceps can cause facial injury/nerve injury. Both can cause serious trauma.

Shoulder dystocia — 1 point

After the head is born, a shoulder becomes trapped behind the pubic bone.

Recognize: Turtle sign—the head retracts against the perineum.

Risk factors: Previous shoulder dystocia, maternal obesity, macrosomia.

PRIORITY: Obtain help and assist with McRoberts positioning and suprapubic pressure, followed by provider-directed maneuvers.

MEMORY TRICK: Turtle → thighs up + suprapubic pressure.

Do not walk into this exam without knowing these

BPP: Heart rate, breathing, movement, tone, fluid; 0 or 2 each.

Anatomy scan = structure; AFP = screening; BPP = well-being.

Polyhydramnios = excess amniotic fluid, not hypertension.

Previa = painless bleeding/no digital vaginal exam; abruption = painful/rigid abdomen. (both can have bleeding which is why they are often compared to one another)

Magnesium toxicity: Stop infusion; calcium gluconate; monitor breathing, reflexes, urine output, and consciousness.

Hyperemesis: Severe vomiting + dehydration/electrolyte problems/weight loss; know the listed risk factors.

GBS: Screen late pregnancy; indicated IV antibiotics during labor.

True labor changes the cervix.

FHR concepts: Early/head, variable/cord, late/placenta; know nursing responses even without reading strips.

Cord prolapse: Lift presenting part off cord, reposition, get help, prepare emergency birth.

Chorio: Fever + uterine tenderness/foul fluid + maternal/fetal tachycardia → antibiotics and monitoring.

Uterine rupture: Prior scar + loss of station/pain → emergency.

Shoulder dystocia: Turtle sign (in and out head) → McRoberts and suprapubic pressure.

Effleurage = light strokes; counterpressure = firm pressure.
