Fluids, Electrolytes & Acid-Base

Balance is everything. Know the normals. Recognize the imbalances. Act before the heart does.

Phase 1 · Foundations

1 · Module Overview

Why this module matters: Electrolyte imbalances are behind some of the most dangerous NCLEX scenarios — cardiac arrest from hyperkalemia, seizures from hyponatremia, respiratory failure from hypomagnesemia. The NCLEX tests whether you can recognize these imbalances early and know which ones are immediately life-threatening.

🎯 What the NCLEX Expects

  • Select the correct IV fluid type (isotonic/hypotonic/hypertonic) for each situation
  • Recognize signs of each electrolyte imbalance
  • Know which imbalances cause cardiac arrhythmias (priority)
  • Identify Chvostek's and Trousseau's signs (hypocalcemia)
  • Know foods high in each electrolyte for teaching
  • Prioritize the most dangerous imbalance when multiple are present
  • Understand third spacing and when to suspect it

🔑 The Golden Rule

Potassium is always the priority electrolyte on the NCLEX. Hyperkalemia → cardiac arrest. Hypokalemia → life-threatening arrhythmias. When K+ is abnormal, the heart is at risk — that always comes first.
Never give IV potassium as a bolus/IV push. This is a high-alert medication. Always dilute and give slowly via pump. Burning at the site is expected — this is normal but can be managed.

2 · Learning Objectives

  • 1Select the correct IV fluid (isotonic, hypotonic, or hypertonic) based on the clinical scenario.
  • 2Identify signs and symptoms of hyponatremia and hypernatremia and the priority nursing actions.
  • 3Recognize hypokalemia and hyperkalemia by ECG changes, symptoms, and causes — and act accordingly.
  • 4Differentiate hypocalcemia from hypercalcemia including Chvostek's and Trousseau's signs.
  • 5Identify the signs of hypomagnesemia and its relationship to other electrolyte imbalances.
  • 6Distinguish fluid volume deficit from fluid volume excess and identify priority assessments.
  • 7Explain third-spacing and identify clinical situations where it occurs.
  • 8Apply the electrolyte priority framework to NCLEX-style prioritization questions.

3 · The Big Picture

🧪

IV Fluids

Isotonic stays in vessels. Hypotonic moves into cells. Hypertonic pulls fluid out of cells.

🧂

Sodium

Na controls water. Low Na → brain swells (seizures). High Na → brain shrinks (confusion). Correct SLOWLY.

⚡

Potassium

K controls cardiac rhythm. Always the #1 priority electrolyte. Never IV push. Correct hypo before giving diuretics.

🦴

Calcium

Low Ca → hyperexcitability (tetany, Chvostek's, Trousseau's). High Ca → everything slows (stones, groans, moans).

💊

Magnesium

Low Mg → hard to correct K & Ca. Check Mg when K stays low. Low Mg → seizures, arrhythmias.

💦

Fluid Balance

FVD → tachycardia first. FVE → crackles, edema, weight gain. Third spacing = fluid trapped where it can't work.

Priority Order for Electrolyte Imbalances Potassium (cardiac) → Sodium (neuro) → Calcium (neuromuscular) → Magnesium (affects everything else). When potassium is abnormal, assess cardiac rhythm first.

🧪 IV Fluid Quick Reference

ISOTONIC (Same as blood)

Examples: 0.9% NS, Lactated Ringer's, D5W (acts isotonic in bag, becomes hypotonic once metabolized)

Use: Fluid replacement, dehydration, blood loss, pre/post-op

Effect: Stays in the vascular space — expands blood volume

⚠️ Caution in HF, renal failure — can cause fluid overload

HYPOTONIC (Less than blood)

Examples: 0.45% NS (half normal saline), 0.33% NS

Use: Cellular dehydration, hypernatremia, DKA (after initial fluid resuscitation)

Effect: Shifts fluid INTO cells — rehydrates cells

⚠️ Never in head injury, burns, or shock — can cause cerebral edema

HYPERTONIC (More than blood)

Examples: 3% NS, D10W, D5 0.9%NS, D5LR, TPN

Use: Severe hyponatremia, cerebral edema, severe burns (later phase)

Effect: Pulls fluid OUT of cells into vessels — shrinks cells

⚠️ Give SLOWLY via pump, central line preferred — monitor for fluid overload

Normal Electrolyte Values — Memorize These
Na⁺: 135–145 mEq/L  |  K⁺: 3.5–5.0 mEq/L  |  Ca²⁺: 8.5–10.5 mg/dL  |  Mg²⁺: 1.5–2.5 mEq/L  |  Phosphorus: 2.5–4.5 mg/dL  |  Cl⁻: 95–105 mEq/L

5 · Deep Dive Lessons

Potassium = #1 NCLEX Priority Electrolyte. Both hypo AND hyperkalemia cause life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias. Know both directions cold.
⬇️ HYPOKALEMIA — K⁺ < 3.5
Causes:Diuretics (thiazide, loop), vomiting, NG suction, diarrhea, poor intake, steroids, insulin
Signs:Muscle weakness/cramps, fatigue, constipation, ↓ bowel sounds, leg cramps, U waves on ECG, flattened T waves, arrhythmias
Priority:Check cardiac rhythm first. Monitor ECG. Assess muscle strength.
Treatment:Oral KCl (preferred if mild), IV KCl if severe (MUST be diluted — never IV push)
Foods:Bananas, oranges, potatoes, avocados, tomatoes, leafy greens, cantaloupe
⚠️ IV K⁺: max 10 mEq/hr peripheral, 20 mEq/hr central. Always via pump. Burning at IV site is expected — can dilute or slow rate.
⬆️ HYPERKALEMIA — K⁺ > 5.0
Causes:Renal failure (can't excrete K), ACE inhibitors, K-sparing diuretics, tissue destruction (burns, rhabdo), acidosis, excess supplementation
Signs:Peaked T waves (first ECG sign), widened QRS, muscle weakness, paresthesias, bradycardia, cardiac arrest
Priority:Cardiac monitoring STAT. Call provider. Prepare to treat immediately.
Treatment:Calcium gluconate (protects heart), sodium bicarbonate, insulin + D50 (drives K into cells), Kayexalate (eliminates K), dialysis
Diet:Restrict: bananas, oranges, potatoes, tomatoes, nuts, salt substitutes (contain KCl)
⚠️ Peaked T waves = CALL PROVIDER NOW. This progresses to ventricular fibrillation rapidly. Never delay cardiac monitoring.
K⁺ + Digoxin = Double Danger Hypokalemia makes digoxin toxicity more likely — low K+ increases myocardial sensitivity to digoxin. Always check K+ before giving digoxin. Hold if K+ <3.5 and notify provider.
Sodium controls water distribution. Where Na goes, water follows. Think BRAIN — both hypo and hypernatremia cause neurological symptoms.
⬇️ HYPONATREMIA — Na⁺ < 135
Causes:SIADH, HF, excessive hypotonic fluids, NG irrigation with water, diuretics, excess water intake
Signs:Headache, confusion, nausea, lethargy, muscle cramps, seizures (severe), coma
Mechanism:Low Na → water moves INTO brain cells → cerebral edema → neuro symptoms
Treatment:Fluid restriction (most common), hypertonic saline (3% NS) for severe, treat underlying cause
⚠️ Correct SLOWLY — rapid correction causes osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS/central pontine myelinolysis). Max 8–12 mEq/L per 24 hours.
⬆️ HYPERNATREMIA — Na⁺ > 145
Causes:Dehydration, diabetes insipidus, excessive Na intake, inability to access water (elderly, confused)
Signs:Thirst, dry sticky mucous membranes, agitation, restlessness, confusion, seizures (severe)
Mechanism:High Na → water moves OUT of brain cells → brain shrinks → neuro symptoms
Treatment:Hypotonic fluids (0.45% NS), free water via NG, treat underlying cause
⚠️ Correct SLOWLY — rapid correction causes cerebral edema. Lower Na no faster than 0.5 mEq/L/hr.
Memory Hook: Both hyponatremia and hypernatremia → neurological symptoms (because the brain is most sensitive to osmotic shifts). Seizure risk is highest with RAPID changes in either direction.
⬇️ HYPOCALCEMIA — Ca²⁺ < 8.5
Causes:Hypoparathyroidism, post-thyroidectomy/parathyroidectomy, vitamin D deficiency, pancreatitis, low Mg
Signs:Tetany, numbness/tingling (circumoral + extremities), muscle cramps, seizures, laryngospasm, prolonged QT
Key Signs:Chvostek's: tap facial nerve → facial twitch. Trousseau's: inflate BP cuff → carpal spasm (more specific)
Treatment:Oral calcium + vitamin D (mild), IV calcium gluconate (severe/symptomatic)
Foods:Dairy, dark leafy greens, sardines, fortified OJ
⚠️ Post-thyroid/parathyroid surgery: assess for hypocalcemia Q4-8h — keep IV calcium gluconate at bedside. Laryngospasm is life-threatening.
⬆️ HYPERCALCEMIA — Ca²⁺ > 10.5
Causes:Hyperparathyroidism, malignancy (bone mets), prolonged immobility, excess vitamin D/calcium, thiazide diuretics
Signs:"Bones, Groans, Psychic Moans, Stones": bone pain, constipation/nausea, confusion/depression, kidney stones
ECG:Shortened QT interval
Treatment:IV fluids (NS — to dilute + promote excretion), loop diuretics, bisphosphonates, calcitonin
⚠️ High fall risk — confusion + muscle weakness. Institute fall precautions. Encourage ambulation to prevent further Ca release from bones.
⬇️ HYPOMAGNESEMIA — Mg²⁺ < 1.5 (Most Common Imbalance in Hospital)
Causes:Alcoholism, malnutrition, diarrhea, NG suction, loop/thiazide diuretics, PPIs long-term
Signs:Tremors, tetany, seizures, positive Chvostek's & Trousseau's, arrhythmias, hyperreflexia
Key Fact:Low Mg causes low K and low Ca that WON'T correct until Mg is replaced first
Treatment:IV magnesium sulfate (monitor for toxicity: loss of DTRs, respiratory depression, bradycardia)
Antidote:Calcium gluconate (for Mg toxicity)
⚠️ Before IV Mg: assess DTRs, RR ≥12, UO ≥25-30 mL/hr. If DTRs absent → hold Mg and call provider.
Rule of Thumb: Think about where you WANT the fluid to go — vessels, cells, or pulling from cells.

🫧 What Is Third Spacing?

Third spacing occurs when fluid shifts from the vascular space into a space where it can't be used for circulation — such as the interstitium, peritoneal cavity, or pleural space. The patient looks like they have enough total body fluid but acts like they're hypovolemic because the fluid is trapped and non-functional.

Common Causes:

  • Burns (massive capillary leak)
  • Peritonitis, bowel obstruction
  • Major abdominal surgery
  • Sepsis (capillary leak syndrome)
  • Pancreatitis
  • Liver failure / ascites
  • Nephrotic syndrome

Signs (Look Like FVD):

  • ↑HR, ↓BP (despite visible edema)
  • ↓UO (fluid not in circulation)
  • Weight gain (fluid is in body, just trapped)
  • Edema, ascites, pleural effusion
  • Normal or low BUN early (dilution effect)
Third Spacing Paradox: The patient has edema AND signs of dehydration at the same time. Don't be fooled by visible swelling — the vascular space is depleted. Treat the hypovolemia with IV fluids (as ordered) while managing the underlying cause.
Fluid Remobilization (Fluid Recovery Phase): After the acute illness resolves, trapped fluid re-enters circulation. Watch for FVE during this phase — lungs fill with fluid that was previously trapped. Monitor for crackles, dyspnea, ↑BP, and distended neck veins during recovery.

⚡ Electrolyte Priority Order (NCLEX)

  1. Potassium (K⁺) — Cardiac arrhythmia risk. ALWAYS assess cardiac rhythm first when K⁺ is abnormal.
  2. Sodium (Na⁺) — Neurological risk (seizures, confusion, brain herniation). Correct slowly in both directions.
  3. Calcium (Ca²⁺) — Neuromuscular risk (tetany, laryngospasm, seizures). Check post-thyroid surgery patients.
  4. Magnesium (Mg²⁺) — Affects ability to correct K and Ca. Check when K stays low despite replacement.
  5. Fluid status — FVD vs. FVE vs. third spacing. Tachycardia is first sign of FVD.
Call Provider Immediately:
K⁺ >6.0 or <2.5 (cardiac emergency)
Na⁺ <120 or >160 (seizure/coma risk)
Ca²⁺ <7.0 (laryngospasm risk)
Peaked T waves on ECG (hyperkalemia)
UO <30 mL/hr × 2 hours (kidney perfusion)
Positive Trousseau's or Chvostek's (hypoCa)

6 · Memory Tricks

Mnemonic

Hypocalcemia: "CT Scan"

Chvostek's (tap Cheek → twitch) · Trousseau's (inflate cuff → carpal Spasm). Trousseau's is more specific — use it to confirm hypocalcemia.

Mnemonic

Hypercalcemia: "Bones, Groans, Psychic Moans, Stones"

Bone pain · Constipation/N&V (groans) · Confusion/depression (psychic moans) · Kidney stones. Everything slows down with too much calcium.

Trick

Hypokalemia: "Flat as a Pancake"

Flat T waves, U waves on ECG. Muscle weakness feels like being flat. Remember: diuretics waste K⁺ — always replace it.

Trick

Hyperkalemia: "Peaked Like a Mountain"

Peaked T waves = first ECG change = DANGER. Think peaked mountain = tall dangerous K. This progresses to V-fib fast.

Trick

IV Fluid Tonicity

ISOtonic = stays in the ISOlated vessel. HYPOtonic = goes INTO cells (HYPOdermic = into). HYPERtonic = pulls fluid OUT (HYPER = overpower the cell).

Trick

Low Mg = Low Everything

When K stays low despite replacement, check Mg. When Ca stays low, check Mg. Hypomagnesemia blocks the correction of both K and Ca — fix Mg first.

Trick

Sodium Correction: SLOW DOWN

Both hyponatremia and hypernatremia must be corrected SLOWLY. Too fast either direction = brain disaster (ODS or cerebral edema). "Slow and steady wins the sodium race."

Trick

Third Spacing = Edema + Dehydration

Patient is swollen but dehydrated at the same time — the fluid is in the wrong compartment. Weight goes UP (fluid in body), BP goes DOWN (fluid not in vessels).

7 · NCLEX Pearls

  • ✅ Potassium is always the #1 priority electrolyte — both hypo and hyperkalemia cause life-threatening cardiac arrhythmias.
  • ✅ Never give IV potassium as a push or bolus — always dilute, always via pump, max 10 mEq/hr peripheral.
  • ✅ Peaked T waves on ECG = hyperkalemia until proven otherwise — notify provider immediately.
  • ✅ Hypokalemia + digoxin = double danger — low K increases digoxin toxicity risk. Check K before every digoxin dose.
  • ✅ Correct sodium slowly in both directions — max 8–12 mEq/L per 24 hours to prevent osmotic demyelination or cerebral edema.
  • ✅ Chvostek's = tap cheek → facial twitch. Trousseau's = inflate BP cuff → carpal spasm. Both = hypocalcemia.
  • ✅ Post-thyroid/parathyroid surgery: keep IV calcium gluconate at bedside — hypocalcemia risk is high.
  • ✅ When K stays low despite replacement, check magnesium — hypomagnesemia prevents K correction.
  • ✅ Daily weight is the most accurate way to monitor fluid status — 1 kg = 1 L of fluid.
  • ✅ Tachycardia is the FIRST sign of fluid volume deficit — precedes hypotension.
  • ✅ D5W acts isotonic in the bag but becomes hypotonic in the body — never use for resuscitation.
  • ✅ Before giving IV magnesium: check DTRs, RR ≥12, UO ≥25 mL/hr — absent DTRs = hold and call.

8 · NCLEX Traps

  • 🚫 Trap: Giving IV K+ by bolus or fast push. Always dilute and use a pump. IV push = cardiac arrest.
  • 🚫 Trap: Using D5W for fluid resuscitation. It's not effective — it becomes free water once metabolized.
  • 🚫 Trap: Giving hypotonic fluids to a patient in shock or with head injury. Causes cerebral edema and worsens hypotension.
  • 🚫 Trap: Rapidly correcting hyponatremia with 3% NS. Causes osmotic demyelination syndrome — correct slowly, max 8–12 mEq/L/24h.
  • 🚫 Trap: Missing hyperkalemia because the patient has edema (looks like FVE). Renal failure patients have both — check K+ on all renal patients.
  • 🚫 Trap: Treating third-spacing edema like regular FVE by restricting fluids. The vascular space is depleted — the patient needs IV fluids, not restriction.
  • 🚫 Trap: Giving K+ to a patient with renal failure without checking urine output. Renal failure = can't excrete K = risk of hyperkalemia.
  • 🚫 Trap: Forgetting to give calcium gluconate before IV magnesium in a patient at risk for toxicity. Calcium gluconate is the antidote for Mg toxicity.
  • 🚫 Trap: Assuming Lactated Ringer's is safe in all patients. It contains potassium — avoid in hyperkalemia. It contains lactate — avoid in liver failure.
  • 🚫 Trap: Thinking a patient with third spacing who is gaining weight is fluid overloaded and needs diuretics. Weight gain = trapped fluid, not circulating fluid — diuretics could worsen the hypovolemia.

9 · Clinical Connections

⚡ Renal Failure Patient

Can't excrete K, Na, or water → hyperkalemia + fluid overload common. Check K every shift. Monitor ECG. Fluid restrict. Avoid K-containing fluids (LR). Dialysis removes what kidneys can't.

💊 Loop Diuretic Patient (Furosemide)

Wastes K, Mg, Ca, Na. Check K before every dose. Supplement K and Mg as ordered. Monitor for muscle weakness, arrhythmias. Weigh daily — 1–2 kg/day loss is goal in HF.

🔥 Burn Patient

Acute phase: massive third spacing → hypovolemia despite swelling. Aggressive IV fluid resuscitation (LR preferred). Monitor UO 30–50 mL/hr. Recovery phase: fluid remobilizes → watch for FVE and pulmonary edema.

🦋 Post-Thyroidectomy

High risk for hypocalcemia (parathyroids removed or damaged). Assess Chvostek's and Trousseau's Q4-8h for 24–48h. Keep calcium gluconate at bedside. Teach signs of tetany before discharge.

🩺 SIADH Patient

Syndrome of inappropriate ADH → water retention → dilutional hyponatremia. Hallmark: Na low, urine concentrated. Treatment: fluid restriction (not saline — worsens it). Demeclocycline or vasopressin antagonists for chronic cases.

🍺 Alcoholic Patient

Classic hypomagnesemia (poor intake + alcohol wastes Mg). May also have hypokalemia, hypocalcemia, hypophosphatemia. Always check Mg on admission. Seizure risk from electrolyte imbalances + alcohol withdrawal — monitor closely.

10 · Priority Framework

💧 Fluids & Electrolytes NCLEX Priority Sequence

  1. Is the heart at risk? Check K⁺ first. ECG changes (peaked T, widened QRS)? Call provider, continuous monitoring.
  2. Is the brain at risk? Sodium abnormal? Assess neuro status — confusion, seizure precautions if Na <125.
  3. Is neuromuscular function at risk? Check Ca²⁺ — tetany, Trousseau's, Chvostek's?
  4. Is Mg blocking correction? K or Ca not correcting with replacement? Check and replace Mg first.
  5. Is fluid status addressed? FVD → isotonic IV fluids, monitor UO. FVE → diuretics, fluid restriction, daily weights.
Report Immediately: K⁺ >6.0 or <2.5 · Na⁺ <120 or >160 · Peaked T waves or widened QRS · UO <30 mL/hr × 2h · Positive Trousseau's post-thyroid surgery · Absent DTRs during IV Mg infusion

11 · Medication Focus

Potassium Chloride (KCl)

Electrolyte Replacement · HIGH ALERT
Use:Hypokalemia replacement — oral or IV
IV Rate:Max 10 mEq/hr peripheral · 20 mEq/hr central · ALWAYS via pump
Monitor:K⁺ levels, ECG, urine output (need UO to excrete excess K)
Teach:Take oral form with food (GI upset); burning at IV site is expected
⚠️ NEVER IV push or bolus — causes cardiac arrest · Never give if UO <30 mL/hr

Magnesium Sulfate (IV)

Electrolyte · Anticonvulsant
Use:Hypomagnesemia, preeclampsia seizure prevention, torsades de pointes
Before:Check DTRs, RR ≥12, UO ≥25 mL/hr before each dose
Toxicity:Loss of DTRs (first sign) → respiratory depression → cardiac arrest
Antidote:Calcium gluconate — keep at bedside during IV Mg infusion
⚠️ Absent DTRs = STOP infusion, call provider · Monitor RR continuously

Calcium Gluconate (IV)

Electrolyte · Cardiac Protectant
Use:Symptomatic hypocalcemia, hyperkalemia (cardiac protection), Mg toxicity antidote
Action:In hyperkalemia: stabilizes cardiac membrane (doesn't lower K — buys time)
Monitor:HR, BP, ECG during infusion; extravasation causes tissue necrosis
Teach:Post-thyroid: take oral calcium with vitamin D for absorption
⚠️ Give slowly — rapid IV causes bradycardia and hypotension · Avoid extravasation

Sodium Polystyrene (Kayexalate)

Cation Exchange Resin · Hyperkalemia
Use:Hyperkalemia — exchanges Na for K in the GI tract to eliminate K in stool
Route:Oral or rectal (enema) — rectal acts faster
Onset:Slow (hours) — not for immediate cardiac emergency (use calcium gluconate first)
Monitor:K⁺ levels, bowel function, Na⁺ (risk of hypernatremia), fluid status
⚠️ Avoid in bowel obstruction · Monitor for constipation or bowel necrosis

13 · NGN Case Studies

Mr. Thompson, 68, has heart failure and is prescribed furosemide 40 mg daily and digoxin 0.125 mg daily. On day 3 after starting furosemide, he complains of muscle cramps and fatigue. Vitals: HR 58, BP 118/74, RR 16, SpO₂ 97%. Labs: K⁺ 2.9, Na⁺ 138, BUN 24, Cr 1.1. His digoxin level is 1.8 ng/mL (therapeutic range 0.5–2.0 ng/mL).
1. What is the priority concern and why?
Priority: hypokalemia (K⁺ 2.9) in a patient on digoxin. Hypokalemia increases myocardial sensitivity to digoxin — even a "therapeutic" digoxin level becomes toxic when K⁺ is low. Signs: muscle cramps, fatigue, bradycardia (HR 58 — may be digoxin effect + low K interaction). This combination is a setup for serious arrhythmias.
2. What nursing actions are priority?
Hold digoxin and notify provider — K⁺ is 2.9 (below 3.5). Place on continuous cardiac monitoring. Administer ordered potassium replacement (oral KCl preferred if able to tolerate; IV if severe). Recheck K⁺ after replacement. Assess for other signs of digoxin toxicity (nausea, visual changes — yellow-green halos, bradycardia). Teach patient high-K foods.
3. The provider orders IV KCl 20 mEq in 100 mL NS over 1 hour. What must the nurse verify before and during administration?
Before: verify IV access is patent and not infiltrated (K extravasation causes tissue damage). Check UO ≥30 mL/hr (need kidneys to excrete excess K). Verify rate — 20 mEq/hr is the peripheral max, so this is at the limit; use a pump. During: monitor cardiac rhythm continuously, assess IV site for burning/redness (expected but manage with slower rate or more dilution), recheck K⁺ 1 hour after completion.
Ms. Rivera, 54, had a total thyroidectomy yesterday for thyroid cancer. It is now post-op day 1. She reports numbness and tingling around her mouth and in her fingertips. She is anxious and says her hands feel like they're "cramping up." Vitals: HR 92, BP 128/82, RR 18, SpO₂ 98%, Temp 37.1°C. Labs are pending. The nurse taps the patient's cheek below the zygomatic bone and notes twitching of the facial muscles.
1. What is happening and what does the facial twitching indicate?
The patient has hypocalcemia — a common complication post-thyroidectomy when the parathyroid glands are accidentally removed or damaged during surgery. Parathyroid hormone (PTH) regulates calcium — without it, Ca²⁺ drops rapidly. The facial twitching when tapping the facial nerve = positive Chvostek's sign, confirming hypocalcemia. The perioral/fingertip tingling and hand cramping are classic neuromuscular excitability signs of low calcium.
2. What is the nurse's priority action?
Notify the provider immediately — symptomatic hypocalcemia requires urgent treatment. Anticipate order for IV calcium gluconate (NOT calcium chloride — gluconate is safer peripherally). Also perform Trousseau's sign (inflate BP cuff above systolic for 3 min — carpal spasm = positive = confirms). Monitor for laryngospasm (life-threatening) — have emergency airway equipment available. Place on continuous cardiac monitoring (hypocalcemia → prolonged QT → arrhythmias).
3. What teaching does Ms. Rivera need before discharge?
Take oral calcium supplements with vitamin D (needed for absorption) as prescribed. Signs to report immediately: muscle cramps, tingling, numbness, difficulty swallowing or breathing (laryngospasm). Take calcium carbonate with meals (acid aids absorption). Avoid high-phosphorus foods (interfere with Ca absorption). Follow-up labs scheduled — Ca²⁺ levels must be monitored closely for weeks. She may need lifelong supplementation if parathyroids were removed.
