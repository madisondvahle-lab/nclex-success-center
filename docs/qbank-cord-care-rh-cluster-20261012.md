## Proposed replacement: #91 — cord stump infection recognition
A parent calls the pediatric clinic about a 9-day-old newborn whose umbilical stump is still attached. The parent reports that the skin around the stump is increasingly red and tender, with foul-smelling yellow discharge. The newborn has been feeding less than usual. What should the nurse advise?
A. Continue dry care only; the stump is expected to remain attached for several more weeks.
B. Apply rubbing alcohol several times daily and reassess in 48 hours.
C. Arrange immediate medical assessment for possible omphalitis.
D. Pull off the loose stump and apply antibiotic ointment.
**Key C.** A delays assessment of infection. B local cleaning is not adequate therapy for suspected omphalitis. **C correct:** spreading erythema, purulent/foul drainage and feeding change require prompt assessment because neonatal infection can progress rapidly. D traction and unsupervised topical therapy are inappropriate.
**Decision:** escalation versus normal healing.

## Proposed replacement: #62 — postpartum RhIg eligibility
An unsensitized mother with confirmed Rh(D)-negative blood delivers an infant whose cord blood is Rh(D)-positive. Delivery occurred 18 hours ago. Which action should the nurse anticipate?
A. Administer Rh(D) immune globulin according to the postpartum protocol, ideally within 72 hours of delivery.
B. Withhold RhIg because the infant is Rh-positive.
C. Give RhIg directly to the newborn to prevent hemolysis.
D. Delay RhIg until the mother's next pregnancy.
**Key A.** **A correct:** postpartum RhIg helps prevent anti-D sensitization in the unsensitized Rh-negative mother after Rh-positive birth. B reverses the indication. C is given to the mother, not the infant for this purpose. D loses the time-sensitive preventive opportunity. Evaluate need for additional dosing if significant fetomaternal hemorrhage.
**Decision:** apply maternal/infant typing and antibody status, not simply recall 72 hours.

## Proposed replacement: #45 — antibody specificity
A pregnant patient at 14 weeks has Rh(D)-negative blood and a positive maternal red-cell antibody screen. Antibody identification has not yet resulted. Which nursing action is most appropriate?
A. Document confirmed anti-D alloimmunization and cancel all further antibody testing.
B. Arrange follow-up of antibody identification and obstetric evaluation before concluding whether RhIg prophylaxis is indicated.
C. Automatically administer RhIg because every positive antibody screen indicates that the patient is unsensitized.
D. Reassure the patient that no further maternal or fetal monitoring can be necessary.
**Key B.** A presumes the specificity of antibodies. **B correct:** an antibody screen may reflect anti-D, another clinically significant antibody, or passive anti-D after prophylaxis, so identity/history and specialist evaluation matter. C confuses prophylaxis with treatment and overlooks sensitization. D dismisses potentially significant alloantibodies.
**Decision:** interpret diagnostic evidence rather than equating a positive screen with immune anti-D.

## Proposed replacement: #109 — Rh incompatibility versus established disease risk
An Rh(D)-negative pregnant patient is carrying an Rh(D)-positive fetus. Maternal antibody testing shows **immune anti-D** and serial titers warrant fetal surveillance. Why is the fetus at risk for hemolytic disease?
A. Maternal anti-D IgG can cross the placenta and destroy Rh(D)-positive fetal erythrocytes.
B. The fetal Rh(D)-positive antigen itself destroys fetal erythrocytes without maternal antibodies.
C. Maternal anti-D antibodies cannot cross the placenta.
D. Rh(D) immune globulin directly treats established fetal hemolysis.
**Key A.** **A correct:** maternal immune anti-D IgG can cross placenta and cause fetal hemolysis. B mere Rh incompatibility does not itself prove disease. C reverses IgG physiology. D RhIg prevents alloimmunization in appropriate unsensitized mothers; it is not treatment for established fetal hemolysis.
**Decision:** connect sensitization/pathophysiology to clinical risk.

## Existing #39 and course #349
Keep dry cord care as a foundational item. Consider updating any narrowly stated 'must fall off in 7–14 days' rationale to indicate timing varies. Some normal spotting around separation is different from sustained bleeding. Avoid suggesting alcohol/antibiotic ointment routinely in usual settings.

## Editorial cautions
- #45's original generalization is especially important: a positive indirect Coombs/antibody screen cannot independently establish **immune anti-D**; verify specificity and history.
- #109's original answer identifies Rh incompatibility, which is a prerequisite but **does not mean the fetus is already affected**. The revision adds a clinically significant anti-D antibody.
- Review choice plausibility; these are first-pass clinical drafts, not approved psychometric items.
