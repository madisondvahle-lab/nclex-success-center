## Proposed option and answer-key changes

The following are draft text proposals for review. Unless stated, the original
stem and correct-answer rationale remain unchanged; rationales must be updated
where an option's meaning changes. Keys refer to the existing A–E option IDs.

### 001 — retain key B; improve distractor plausibility and length balance

Keep correct option B and its technique rationale. Replace A, C, and D with:

- **A:** “Test both eyes together at the chart's specified distance with usual correction, then record one Snellen fraction.”
- **C:** “Test each eye separately without the patient's usual distance correction, then compare the results with the corrected baseline.”
- **D:** “Test the right eye at a shorter distance and convert the result to the chart's standard distance without noting the change.”

These represent common errors in monocular comparison, correction, and
documentation without making the correct answer artificially short. Recheck
that each alternative remains clearly incorrect in the context of the chart
protocol.

### 002 — proposed key C

Replace the options with parallel interpretations:

- **A:** “At 50 feet, the patient sees what a person with standard acuity sees at 20 feet.”
- **B:** “At 20 feet, the patient reads the 20/20 line but has difficulty reading a near card.”
- **C:** “At 20 feet, the patient reads what a person with standard acuity reads at 50 feet.”
- **D:** “The result localizes the reduced acuity to the retina rather than simply describing visual performance.”

**Proposed rationale:** The numerator is the testing distance and the
denominator is the distance at which standard acuity reads the same line.
Thus, 20/50 indicates reduced distance acuity; the fraction alone does not
identify its cause.

### 004 — proposed key ACE

Retain the direct-light and near-response concepts. Revise B and E:

- **B:** “Only the illuminated pupil should constrict when light is directed into one eye.”
- **E:** “The opposite pupil also constricts when light is directed into one eye.”

**Proposed rationale:** Light in one eye produces direct constriction in the
illuminated pupil and consensual constriction in the opposite pupil. Near focus
normally includes convergence and pupillary constriction. A is direct response,
C is near response, and E is consensual response.

### 005 — proposed key B

Replace choices with parallel interpretations of the corneal light reflex:

- **A:** “A symmetric reflection suggests normal acuity and peripheral fields in both eyes.”
- **B:** “Symmetric reflections support aligned eye position in primary gaze but do not assess acuity or fields.”
- **C:** “A symmetric reflex is enough to assess extraocular movement through each gaze position.”
- **D:** “Assess the reflex while the patient follows the penlight through the six gaze positions.”

**Proposed rationale:** A symmetric corneal light reflex supports alignment in
primary gaze. It does not establish acuity, fields, or full extraocular
movement.

### 007 — proposed key D; resolve priority ambiguity

Retain the acute angle-closure symptom cluster. Replace the response choices:

- **A:** “Complete acuity, field, and pupil testing in both eyes, then contact the receiving clinician with results.”
- **B:** “Arrange routine eye evaluation later that day, and advise the patient to return sooner if vision worsens.”
- **C:** “Use an over-the-counter redness-relief drop, reassess symptoms, and escalate only if they persist.”
- **D:** “Initiate immediate emergency evaluation; obtain and communicate focused findings without delaying escalation.”

**Proposed rationale:** Severe unilateral pain, red eye, halos/blurred vision,
and nausea are concerning for acute angle closure and require immediate
emergency evaluation. Focused assessment supports handoff but must not delay
escalation.

### 009 — proposed key C

Replace choices with parallel screening/follow-up responses:

- **A:** “A symptom-free history is enough to defer retinal screening until a new visual change is reported.”
- **B:** “A normal pupil response is reassuring enough to choose a less frequent retinal-screening interval.”
- **C:** “Yes. Early retinal changes may be silent; follow the dilated-exam interval recommended by the eye-care clinician.”
- **D:** “A routine central-acuity check is a suitable substitute for a dilated retinal examination in an asymptomatic patient.”

**Proposed rationale:** Early diabetic retinopathy may have no symptoms.
Screening is therefore not replaced by a symptom-free report or a normal pupil
response. The proposed wording appropriately refers to the patient's
recommended schedule rather than making a new universal interval claim.

### 010 — proposed key ABCE

Retain A, B, and C in substance. Replace D and add E:

- **D:** “If the redness improves briefly after an old prescription drop, routine follow-up is sufficient.”
- **E:** “If it does not delay referral, assess and document visual acuity in each eye.”

**Proposed rationale:** Pain, photophobia, and blurred vision in a
contact-lens wearer require prompt assessment for potentially vision-threatening
corneal disease. Focused history, acuity assessment when feasible, and stopping
lens wear are appropriate, but none should delay referral. Do not use leftover
or another person's medication as a substitute for evaluation.

### 012 — proposed key BCDE

Rewrite options to avoid a giveaway absolute and distinguish possibility from
diagnosis:

- **A:** “Itching after yard work most strongly suggests viral conjunctivitis rather than allergy.”
- **B:** “Ask about discharge, sick contacts, exposures, and whether one or both eyes are affected.”
- **C:** “Use hand hygiene and avoid sharing eye cosmetics or towels while the cause is being clarified.”
- **D:** “Antibiotics are not indicated solely because an eye is red; treatment depends on the likely cause and assessment.”
- **E:** “Redness alone cannot distinguish allergic, viral, bacterial, or irritant causes.”

**Proposed rationale:** Itching and exposure support allergy as a possibility,
not a confirmed diagnosis. Focused history helps characterize the cause;
hygiene and avoiding shared personal items are prudent while infection remains
possible. Redness alone does not determine etiology or treatment.

### 013 — proposed key D; difficulty `moderate`

Replace choices:

- **A:** “Record the difference as a common variation if the child has no visual complaint or discomfort.”
- **B:** “Use any normal distance-acuity result, if obtainable, as enough to dismiss the asymmetric reflex.”
- **C:** “Ask whether it is a longstanding difference, and defer referral unless the caregiver reports a change.”
- **D:** “Confirm the finding with correct technique, compare both eyes, document the asymmetry, and arrange prompt eye-care evaluation.”

**Proposed rationale:** An absent or asymmetric red reflex is an abnormal
screening finding that requires prompt evaluation. A child need not be able to
complete a Snellen chart or report symptoms for the finding to matter.

### 014 — proposed key B; difficulty `moderate`

Replace choices with competing bedside screening techniques:

- **A:** “Have the patient read a distance chart with both eyes open and use the result to map the peripheral field.”
- **B:** “Test one eye at a time and compare the patient's detection of a peripheral target with the examiner's field.”
- **C:** “Test one eye at a time using a central near-vision card; central detail is the bedside screen for peripheral loss.”
- **D:** “Compare direct pupillary constriction in each eye to identify the location of a peripheral-field defect.”

**Proposed rationale:** Confrontation screening compares peripheral-target
detection with the examiner while testing one eye at a time. Acuity and
pupillary-response tests measure different functions.

### 016 — proposed key ABE

Retain A and B in substance. Replace C–E:

- **C:** “A normal central Snellen result makes a peripheral-field change less likely.”
- **D:** “The symptom pattern strongly supports chronic open-angle glaucoma and can establish the diagnosis.”
- **E:** “A comprehensive eye examination, including visual-field assessment, is needed; a central acuity result alone cannot exclude field loss.”

**Proposed rationale:** Chronic open-angle glaucoma may progress slowly,
beginning with peripheral-field loss, and may be painless. The symptom pattern
does not diagnose glaucoma, and normal central acuity does not clear a
peripheral-field complaint.

### 017 — proposed key D; difficulty `moderate`

Replace choices:

- **A:** “The tender, red eyelid-margin lesion is more consistent with a chalazion; the persistent painless lump is more consistent with a hordeolum.”
- **B:** “Both findings are caused by bacterial infection of an eyelid gland, differing mainly in duration.”
- **C:** “Most eyelid lesions resolve, so persistence is less relevant than tenderness when deciding on evaluation.”
- **D:** “A tender, acute eyelid-margin lesion fits a hordeolum; a persistent, usually painless lump within the lid fits a chalazion.”

**Proposed rationale:** A hordeolum is typically an acute, tender localized
infection. A chalazion is usually a chronic, noninfectious inflammatory lump
related to gland obstruction. Persistent, recurrent, or vision-affecting lesions
need evaluation. This wording preserves the existing clinical correction to
the lecture's overstatement that chalazia are infections.

### 018 — proposed key BDE

Revise to distribute correct actions and replace implausible distractors:

- **A:** “Keep the room brightly lit so the pupils remain constricted during the examination.”
- **B:** “Explain the close approach before bringing the instrument near the patient.”
- **C:** “Ask the patient to track the ophthalmoscope as it moves across the fundus.”
- **D:** “Use the examiner's matching eye and hand for the corresponding eye and approach from the temporal side.”
- **E:** “Ask the patient to keep gaze on a distant fixed point while the fundus is viewed.”

**Proposed rationale:** Explain the close approach, align the examiner's eye
and hand with the side being examined, and have the patient maintain distant
fixation. A dim room can facilitate pupil observation; tracking the instrument
or using bright light to constrict the pupil makes the view more difficult.

### 019 — proposed key C; difficulty `moderate`

Replace the choices to remove absolutes and use parallel descriptions of eye
findings:

- **A:** “A cataract-related opacity within the lens, usually seen as clouding through the pupil.”
- **B:** “A pinguecula, a localized conjunctival change beside the cornea rather than a corneal ring.”
- **C:** “Corneal arcus, a gray-white ring at the corneal edge that can be age-associated in older adults.”
- **D:** “A corneal abrasion, a surface injury that can appear as a complete gray-white peripheral ring.”

**Proposed rationale:** Arcus is a gray-white peripheral corneal ring and may
be age-associated. Document its location and interpret it in context; the
finding does not by itself explain a separate visual complaint.

### 020 — proposed key ACD; reduce repetition with #006 and #007

Replace the stem with: “A patient reports a sudden visual change that is being
urgently escalated. Which nursing actions support the handoff without delaying
evaluation? Select all that apply.”

- **A:** “Document when the change began and whether it was sudden, intermittent, or worsening.”
- **B:** “Wait for a complete routine eye examination before notifying the receiving clinician.”
- **C:** “Describe which eye and which part of the visual field are affected.”
- **D:** “Communicate associated flashes, floaters, pain, redness, halos, nausea, and any acuity findings already obtained.”
- **E:** “Ask the patient to drive to the eye clinic if symptoms begin to improve.”

**Proposed rationale:** Onset, affected eye/field, associated symptoms, and
available acuity findings support urgent handoff. Do not delay escalation for a
complete exam or have a patient with sudden visual change drive themselves.
This tests nursing response and communication rather than repeating the red-flag
recognition in #006 and #007.
