## #22: IV heparin, protocol-defined aPTT
An adult receiving IV unfractionated heparin for pulmonary embolism has an aPTT of 180 seconds. The hospital protocol defines a therapeutic range of 60–90 seconds. Which action should the nurse take?
A. Continue infusion and recheck in six hours.
B. Increase the infusion rate.
C. Hold infusion per protocol, assess for bleeding and notify the responsible clinician.
D. Give protamine without an order.
**Key C.** A delays response to excess anticoagulation. B increases bleeding risk. C respects the local protocol and evaluates hemorrhage. D reversal is not automatically necessary for an isolated laboratory value. No universal aPTT range applies to all assays.

## #111: Burn resuscitation
A 70-kg adult with burns affecting 40% TBSA is receiving protocol-guided lactated Ringer's. Six hours after injury urine output is 15 mL in the past hour, HR 122/min, BP 94/56 mm Hg. What is most appropriate?
A. Keep the initial formula rate unchanged.
B. Notify the burn team and adjust fluids according to resuscitation protocol, reassessing perfusion and urine output.
C. Restrict IV fluids because edema is expected.
D. Give furosemide solely to increase urine output.
**Key B.** A ignores changing needs; B addresses inadequate urine output versus an approximate goal of 35 mL/h, with perfusion-based reassessment; C risks under-resuscitation; D risks masking insufficient circulating volume. Formula estimates are initial guides, not fixed orders.
Reference: https://doi.org/10.1093/jbcr/irad125

## #178: Adrenal crisis
An adult with primary adrenal insufficiency has vomited for three days, is confused and cannot take oral medication. BP 76/42 mm Hg. Which treatment should the nurse prepare immediately?
A. Oral fludrocortisone with reassessment in an hour.
B. Parenteral hydrocortisone plus rapid isotonic IV fluid resuscitation as prescribed.
C. Wait for cortisol results before treating.
D. Restrict IV fluids.
**Key B.** A is inadequate in shock and oral intolerance; B addresses steroid deficiency and hypovolemia; C delays life-saving therapy; D worsens circulatory compromise.
Reference: https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/primary-adrenal-insufficiency

## #227: SIADH and worsening symptomatic hyponatremia
A patient with SIADH has sodium decrease from 128 to 118 mEq/L over 24 hours, with new confusion and vomiting. What is the priority?
A. Encourage free-water intake.
B. Institute seizure/fall precautions, urgently escalate to the clinician and prepare for monitored treatment of symptomatic hyponatremia.
C. Give hypotonic IV fluid.
D. Reassess sodium tomorrow.
**Key B.** A and C can further reduce sodium; B recognizes neurologic deterioration and the need for urgent evaluation, with therapy such as prescribed monitored hypertonic saline depending on clinical assessment; D dangerously delays action. Avoid teaching seizure precautions as definitive treatment. Review hyponatremia guidance before approval.

## #103: Preeclampsia rationale correction
Retain the original stem and key C provisionally. Proposed rationale: New-onset hypertension after 20 weeks combined with severe headache and marked proteinuria warrants urgent assessment for preeclampsia. Proteinuria is NOT essential for diagnosis when qualifying organ dysfunction or severe features are present. Dipstick results alone do not determine severity. The question should also change its opening phrase, because a WBC count is not a urinalysis finding.
