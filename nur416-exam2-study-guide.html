Chapter 16: Sensory Function

Identify normal and abnormal assessment findings in common problems & conditions in hearing and balance.

Normal age-related changes

Presbycusis: gradual, bilateral sensorineural hearing loss associated with aging.

High-frequency sounds are typically lost first.

Difficulty distinguishing speech, especially with background noise.

Cerumen tends to become drier and harder, increasing risk for impaction.

Some decline in vestibular function can contribute to impaired balance.

Abnormal / concerning

Sudden hearing loss

Unilateral hearing loss

New tinnitus, especially unilateral

Ear pain or drainage

Severe/new vertigo

Hearing loss associated with neurologic findings

Nursing communication

Face the patient.

Reduce background noise.

Speak clearly and slowly in a normal/lower-pitched voice.

Do not shout.

Make sure hearing aids are functioning.

Confirm understanding rather than assuming the patient heard you.

Remember: Older adults commonly lose high-frequency hearing first.

Identify normal and abnormal assessment findings in common problems & conditions in vision.

Expected aging changes

Presbyopia: decreased ability to focus on near objects.

Smaller pupils and slower adaptation to changes in light.

Increased need for illumination.

Decreased contrast sensitivity.

More difficulty with glare/night vision.

Common disorders

Cataracts

Clouding of the lens

Gradual, painless vision loss

Blurred/cloudy vision

Glare/halos around lights

Colors may appear faded

Glaucoma

Increased optic nerve damage, often associated with increased intraocular pressure.

Open-angle glaucoma may initially be silent.

Progressive peripheral vision loss.

Acute angle-closure symptoms are an emergency: severe eye pain, headache, halos, nausea/vomiting.

Macular degeneration

Primarily affects central vision.

Peripheral vision may remain intact.

Difficulty reading and recognizing faces.

Memory trick

Glaucoma = peripheral

Macular = middle/central

Cataract = cloudy

Differentiate between retinal disorders and nursing interventions associated with each.

Retinal detachment

Flashes of light

New floaters

"Curtain" or shadow across visual field

Emergency evaluation required

Diabetic retinopathy

Damage to retinal blood vessels from diabetes.

May initially be asymptomatic.

Prevention/progression reduction focuses heavily on glucose and BP control and routine dilated eye exams.

Macular degeneration

Central vision loss.

Dry form is more common and progresses more slowly.

Wet form involves abnormal blood vessel growth/leakage and can progress rapidly.

Sudden flashes + floaters + curtain = retinal detachment until proven otherwise.

Notes

Identify nursing care guidelines for xerostomia.

Xerostomia = dry mouth

Common contributors:

Medications, especially those with anticholinergic effects

Dehydration

Reduced salivary production

Radiation involving salivary glands

Care

Frequent sips of water

Good oral hygiene

Sugar-free gum/candy if appropriate to stimulate saliva

Saliva substitutes/artificial saliva

Avoid tobacco and alcohol-containing mouthwash

Regular dental care

Dry mouth increases risk for dental caries, oral infection, difficulty swallowing, and poor nutrition.

Identify age-related changes in regard to touch.

Aging may cause:

↓ tactile sensitivity

↓ ability to detect vibration/pressure

↓ temperature discrimination

Slower sensory processing

Safety implication: An older adult may not recognize excessive heat/cold as quickly, increasing risk for burns and injury.

Chapter 17: Cardiovascular Function

Understand patient teachings for older adults with a pacemaker.

Teach the patient to:

Monitor pulse as instructed.

Keep follow-up appointments for device checks.

Carry pacemaker identification information.

Report dizziness, syncope, palpitations, dyspnea, or persistent hiccups.

Follow restrictions after implantation regarding the affected arm as prescribed while the leads heal.

Avoid placing strong magnets directly over the device.

Tell healthcare providers about the pacemaker before procedures.

Possible pacemaker malfunction symptoms: dizziness, syncope, fatigue, bradycardia, palpitations.

Chapter 18: Respiratory Function

Asthma in older adults

Older adults may have multiple comorbidities and may have difficulty using inhalers correctly.

Nursing care:

Assess inhaler technique.

Identify triggers.

Monitor respiratory status.

Encourage adherence to controller medications.

Rescue inhaler = rapid symptom relief.

Controller medications are used for long-term control.

Important: Beta-agonists can cause tachycardia/tremor, which may be particularly relevant in older adults with cardiovascular disease.

Pneumonia in older adults

Classic symptoms may be less obvious.

An older adult may present with:

New confusion/delirium

Weakness

Falls

Poor appetite

Functional decline

Tachypnea

Fever may be absent or less pronounced

Care may include:

Antibiotics when bacterial infection is diagnosed/suspected as appropriate

Oxygen as needed

Hydration

Pulmonary hygiene

Mobility

Vaccination/prevention strategies

Exam clue: Sudden confusion in an older adult → look for an underlying physiologic cause, including infection.

Tuberculosis risk factors

Risk increases with:

Previous TB exposure/infection

Immunosuppression

Congregate living environments

Close contact with active TB

Certain chronic illnesses

Residence/travel in high-prevalence areas

Active pulmonary TB: airborne precautions.

Notes

Obstructive Sleep Apnea

Symptoms

Loud snoring

Witnessed apneas

Daytime sleepiness

Morning headaches

Poor concentration

Diagnosis: polysomnography/sleep study.

Treatment

CPAP is common first-line therapy for many patients.

Weight management when appropriate.

Avoid sedating substances/medications when clinically appropriate.

COPD in older adults

Common findings:

Chronic cough

Sputum production

Dyspnea

Exercise intolerance

Decreased breath sounds

Prolonged expiration

Care:

Smoking cessation

Bronchodilators

Inhaled therapies as prescribed

Vaccination

Pulmonary rehabilitation/activity

Nutrition support when needed

Oxygen when indicated

Goal: Optimize oxygenation while monitoring the patient's respiratory status and response to therapy.

H. pylori

Smoking

Think:

Chapter 21: Musculoskeletal Function

Hip fracture

Common findings:

Pain

Inability to bear weight

Affected leg may be shortened and externally rotated

Older adults are at high risk for complications related to immobility:

DVT

Pneumonia

Pressure injuries

Delirium

Functional decline

Priority: pain control, safe mobility, prevention of complications, and early rehabilitation/mobilization as appropriate.

Osteomyelitis

Bone infection.

Possible findings:

Localized bone pain/tenderness

Swelling/warmth

Fever may occur

Drainage if chronic/open wound

Treatment often requires prolonged antimicrobial therapy and sometimes surgical management.

Foot disorders

Corn: localized thickened skin over pressure/friction area.

Callus: broader area of thickened skin.

Bunion: bony prominence/deformity at the first MTP joint.

Hammertoe: abnormal flexion/bending of toe joint.

Older adults, particularly those with diabetes or impaired circulation, should avoid self-treating/cutting foot lesions because of injury/infection risk.

Notes

Osteoarthritis

Think wear-and-tear / degenerative joint disease.

Pain worsens with use/activity

Brief morning stiffness

Crepitus

Reduced ROM

Bony enlargement

Management:

Activity/exercise

Weight management when appropriate

Heat/cold

Analgesics/anti-inflammatory therapy when appropriate

Assistive devices if needed

Gout

Uric acid crystal deposition

Classic:

Sudden severe joint pain

Red, hot, swollen joint

Often first MTP/big toe

Acute attack: anti-inflammatory treatment.

Long-term prevention: urate-lowering therapy such as allopurinol when indicated.

Allopurinol is primarily for long-term uric acid control, not immediate pain relief during an acute attack.

Osteoporosis

Common therapies may include:

Bisphosphonates

Calcium/vitamin D when appropriate

Weight-bearing/resistance exercise

Fall prevention

Bisphosphonate teaching

Take according to specific product instructions, commonly first thing in the morning with plain water.

Remain upright for at least 30 minutes for many oral agents.

Avoid food/other medications during the specified absorption period.

Why? → Reduce esophageal irritation and improve absorption.

Medication selection requires caution because older adults are more susceptible to:

Chapter 26: Cancer

Lung cancer risk factors

Major risk:

Tobacco exposure

Others:

Secondhand smoke

Radon

Occupational/environmental carcinogens

Prior radiation/exposures depending on history

Colorectal cancer

Risk increases with:

Age

Family/personal history

Certain hereditary syndromes

Inflammatory bowel disease

Lifestyle factors

Screening decisions in older adults should consider age, overall health, prior screening history, life expectancy, and patient preferences, rather than age alone.

Chemotherapy-induced oral mucositis

Care:

Frequent oral assessment

Gentle oral hygiene

Soft toothbrush

Bland rinses as directed

Maintain hydration/nutrition

Avoid irritating/spicy/acidic foods if painful

Monitor for infection

Severe mucositis can interfere with nutrition, hydration, and infection prevention.

Radiation therapy

External radiation

Patient is NOT radioactive after treatment.

Common care:

Protect treatment-site skin.

Avoid unapproved creams/lotions.

Do not remove treatment markings unless instructed.

Protect area from sun/extreme temperatures.

Fatigue is common.

Chapter 27: Loss & End-of-Life Issues

Grief responses

Grief varies considerably between individuals.

May include:

Anticipatory grief

Normal grief

Complicated/prolonged grief

Disenfranchised grief

There is no single correct timetable for grieving.

Nursing care focuses on listening, supporting coping, respecting cultural/spiritual practices, and assessing for safety concerns or impaired functioning.

Increasing sleep

Decreased appetite/thirst

Decreased urine output

Weakness

Cool/mottled extremities

Decreased responsiveness

Skin care

Respect cultural/spiritual wishes

Avoid unnecessary burdensome interventions when inconsistent with goals of care

Hospice nurse role

Hospice emphasizes comfort and quality of life rather than curative treatment of the terminal illness.

The nurse may:

Manage pain and other symptoms

Educate patient/family

Provide psychosocial support

Coordinate interdisciplinary care

Support spiritual/cultural preferences

Assist family through the dying process

Provide/support bereavement care

Hospice does NOT mean "do nothing." It means the goals of treatment have changed.
